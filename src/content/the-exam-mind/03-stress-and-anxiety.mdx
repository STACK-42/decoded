# Stress & Anxiety

You studied. You slept. You knew the material the night before. Then you sat down in that room, read the first question, and something happened. The material seemed to go somewhere else, or start to fade. You knew that you knew it, but it was not there, and you lost your grip on what you thought you understood. You started overthinking it, and fearing it.

Once you understand what happened there, exams feel different, and so does the way you prepare for them.

## Introducing Cortisol

When your brain detects a threat, whether it is a dog running toward you or an exam paper, it triggers the release of cortisol. **Cortisol** is a steroid hormone produced by your **adrenal glands**, two small hormone glands sitting on top of each kidney. Its job is to prepare your body to deal with an emergency: heart rate climbs, blood pressure rises, and blood flow shifts toward your muscles while digestion slows down. You feel restless or tense as your attention narrows onto the threat.

One way to learn to spot cortisol is by logging your emotions. I have been using the [How We Feel app](https://howwefeel.org/) for around four years now, and it took me years to connect "restless" and "tense" to cortisol. An Apple Watch helped too, since some apps track your stress levels. With both together, I got much better at noticing when I am stressed and bringing it down.

Nothing about that system is broken. It kept your ancestors alive. It was built for physical danger. When the threat is a predator, narrowed focus and muscle readiness are exactly what you need. When the threat is a multi-step proof or a calculus problem, they work against you.

The **prefrontal cortex**, the front part of your brain responsible for complex reasoning, [working memory](/courses/the-exam-mind/the-one-week-plan#before-you-plan-anything-build-the-map), rule switching, and deliberate recall, is sensitive to stress hormones. Under acute stress, high levels of catecholamines like norepinephrine and dopamine flood the brain alongside cortisol. In moderate amounts they sharpen focus, but at high levels they bind to lower-affinity receptors in the prefrontal cortex. That binding disconnects neural connections and disrupts working memory buffers. Control shifts subcortically to the amygdala, the brain's emotional alarm center, which favors fast, habitual reactions over careful analysis.

The prefrontal cortex is not damaged and no knowledge is erased. It is temporarily **suppressed**. Your brain is running a survival program inside a classroom. This temporary stress-induced retrieval block is what students call **blanking**.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Usual mistake: panicking when your mind goes blank">
When your mind suddenly feels empty on a question you studied, students often assume the knowledge is completely gone. That panic triggers another spike of adrenaline, which floods the prefrontal cortex even further. The knowledge is still intact in long-term storage; only the retrieval pathway is clogged by high cortisol. Put your pen down, skip to a problem you know how to solve, and give your stress levels 60 seconds to drop.
</Callout>

## Balancing Extremes of Anxiety

Cortisol does not simply make things worse as it rises. The relationship between stress and performance follows what psychologists call the **Yerkes-Dodson curve**, an inverted U.

<LessonImage src="https://res.cloudinary.com/dkmutnahg/image/upload/v1778893904/graph.002_ayktx3.png" alt="Yerkes-Dodson curve" width={1366} height={432} />
[Image Source: Diamond D.M., et al. (2007)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1906714/)

With too little stress, performance is poor because arousal is low: you feel sluggish, unmotivated, and inattentive. With excessive stress, performance collapses because the prefrontal cortex is overwhelmed by stress hormones. Peak performance sits in the middle: enough pressure to sharpen attention, but not enough to trigger survival panic.

The optimal peak depends on the task. Simple physical tasks like running tolerate high arousal. Complex intellectual tasks that demand multi-step reasoning, such as a computer science or engineering exam, have a lower threshold: working memory degrades early under high stress.

A small amount of exam anxiety is an asset because it mobilizes energy and sharpens concentration. Trouble starts when a student interprets normal physical arousal as panic, triggering a self-reinforcing stress loop.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Usual mistake: mistaking physical arousal for panic">
A racing heartbeat, quickened breathing, and cold hands before an exam are not signs that you are failing. They are your sympathetic nervous system delivering oxygen and glucose so your brain can work. If you tell yourself "I am panicking," your brain generates more fear. If you tell yourself "my body is preparing to focus," performance stays high.
</Callout>

## Negativity Bias

A second mental habit makes exam stress worse: the **negativity bias**.

Your brain does not treat all events equally. It gives mistakes, threats, and unpleasant experiences more weight and memory space than positive ones. In evolutionary history, missing a threat (such as a predator) meant death, whereas missing a positive reward (such as an extra berry bush) was harmless. The amygdala tags threatening and erroneous events with emotional salience and stress hormones, leading to rapid storage of perceived failures while neutral successes pass by unnoticed.

In an exam room, that bias distorts your judgment. You might finish an exam having solved nineteen questions well and one question poorly. On the walk home, you replay the one missed question repeatedly. You reconstruct your error, overthink it, and worry that you failed the entire course. The nineteen successful answers sit in memory too, but because solving them felt smooth, they carry almost no emotional weight.

Knowing about this bias allows you to catch it before it shapes your mood.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Usual mistake: recalculating your grade on the walk home">
Leaving the exam room and immediately calculating your worst-case score based on the one problem you could not solve. Your brain naturally hides the questions that went smoothly and highlights the one that went wrong.
</Callout>

<Callout tone="violet" label="The negativity bias in real time">
When you leave an exam convinced you failed, ask yourself: *Am I remembering everything, or only what went wrong?* Your brain will always make the negative more available than the positive, so account for that before you reach a conclusion.
</Callout>

## "I Have Never Seen This Before"

This is where most exam marks are lost. You hit a question that looks completely unfamiliar, and your immediate reaction is shock.

What happens next is usually the wrong move: freezing in place, spending 25 minutes staring at that single problem, or deciding the exam is lost and rushing the rest.

Exams from serious university courses almost always contain questions that most students cannot fully solve. Instructors use baseline questions to confirm core competence, intermediate problems to assess mastery, and stretch questions to separate top grades. A question you cannot solve immediately is not a trick or a sign of personal failure. It is a normal part of exam design.

When you encounter an unfamiliar question, use this five-step triage protocol:

1. **Put down your pen for five seconds:** Stop writing immediately. This physical pause interrupts the impulsive urge to scribble random equations.
2. **Identify the topic cluster (30 seconds):** Check your mental map. What general topic does this belong to? Write down any relevant definitions, governing formulas, or given variables in the margin.
3. **Apply the 60-second rule:** If no viable solution path appears after one minute, stop working on it. Draw a clear mark (such as a circle or question mark) next to the problem number.
4. **Turn the page immediately:** Move to the next problem. Do not let your gaze linger on the stuck question.
5. **Return at the end:** After you have banked every straightforward mark on the paper, return to the marked problems with your remaining time. You will tackle them with lower cortisol, and you can harvest partial credit by writing down known principles and logical assumptions.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Usual mistake: fighting a stuck problem on page one">
Spending 20 minutes wrestling with an unfamiliar question on page one while thirty marks of straightforward questions sit unread at the back of the paper. You lose both time and working memory capacity.
</Callout>

<Callout tone="mint" label="The reframe that actually helps">
An unfamiliar question means the exam is doing its job, and says little about how prepared you are. Your job is to do well on everything else and not let one hard question borrow time and attention from the ones you can answer.
</Callout>

## What You Can Actually Do

**Before the exam:** The most effective defense against cortisol is disciplined preparation. Regular [retrieval practice](/courses/the-exam-mind/the-problem-with-how-you-study#how-memory-actually-works) builds genuine confidence grounded in evidence of what you know. Reliable sleep the night before protects working memory.

**In the exam room:** If you feel an acute spike of anxiety, activate the **parasympathetic nervous system**, the branch of the autonomic nervous system that acts as an internal brake, slowing heart rate and lowering blood pressure.

The fastest way to engage this brake is the **physiological sigh**, a breathing pattern studied extensively by neurobiologists:

1. Take a deep, continuous inhale through your nose for about two seconds.
2. Without exhaling, take a second, sharp sip of air through your nose to fully expand your lungs. This second inhale pops open collapsed **alveoli**, the tiny air sacs in your lungs where carbon dioxide builds up during shallow stress breathing.
3. Exhale slowly and completely through your mouth for six to eight seconds.
4. Repeat this cycle two or three times.

The long exhalation increases pressure in your chest cavity, causing the heart to expand slightly. Sensory nerves detect that expansion and signal the brainstem via the vagus nerve to slow the heart rate down. It is silent, takes twenty seconds at your desk, and measurably lowers physical tension.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Usual mistake: rapid deep chest breathing">
Trying to calm down by taking rapid, forced chest breaths. Hyperventilating expels too much carbon dioxide too fast, which constricts blood vessels in the brain, causes lightheadedness, and amplifies feelings of panic.
</Callout>

**After the exam:** Put an objective number on your negativity bias. Ask yourself: *How many marks did I handle reasonably well?* Write down an honest estimate of the marks you banked. It will almost always be higher than your emotional reaction suggests.
