# Stress & Anxiety

You studied the material and slept well the night before. But when you sit down, read the first question, and try to recall an answer, your mind goes blank. The concepts you felt confident about yesterday suddenly feel out of reach, and second-guessing sets in.

Understanding how your body reacts to stress explains why recall fails under pressure, and how to stay in control during an exam.

## Introducing Cortisol

When your brain detects a threat, whether an aggressive dog or a difficult exam paper, it triggers the release of cortisol. **Cortisol** is a steroid hormone produced by the **adrenal glands**, two small glands that sit on top of each kidney. Its job is to prepare your body for an emergency: heart rate climbs, blood pressure rises, and blood flow shifts toward your muscles while digestion slows down. You feel restless or tense as your attention narrows onto the threat.

One way to spot cortisol is by logging emotions. I have used the [How We Feel app](https://howwefeel.org/) for four years, and tracking my feelings helped me connect sensations like restlessness and tension to cortisol spikes. Wearables like an Apple Watch can track physical stress as well. Using both made it much easier to notice stress early and calm down before it peaks.

This response evolved for physical danger. Narrowed focus and muscle readiness help when escaping a predator, but they work against you when solving a multi-step proof or a calculus problem.

The **prefrontal cortex**, the front region of your brain responsible for complex reasoning, [working memory](/courses/the-exam-mind/the-one-week-plan#before-you-plan-anything-build-the-map), rule switching, and deliberate recall, is sensitive to stress hormones. Under acute stress, high levels of catecholamines like norepinephrine and dopamine flood the brain alongside cortisol. In moderate amounts they sharpen focus, but at high levels they bind to lower-affinity receptors in the prefrontal cortex. That binding disrupts working memory buffers and weakens neural signaling. Control shifts to the amygdala, the brain's emotional alarm center, which favors fast, reflexive habits over careful analysis.

Stress does not damage the prefrontal cortex or erase stored knowledge; it temporarily suppresses active retrieval. This temporary retrieval block is what students call **blanking**.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Usual mistake: panicking when your mind goes blank">
When your mind suddenly feels empty on a question you studied, you might assume the knowledge is gone. That panic triggers another spike of adrenaline, which suppresses the prefrontal cortex even further. Your knowledge remains in long-term storage; high cortisol has only blocked the retrieval pathway. Put your pen down, skip to a problem you know how to solve, and give your stress levels 60 seconds to drop.
</Callout>

## Balancing Extremes of Anxiety

Stress does not harm performance at every level. The relationship between stress and performance follows the **Yerkes-Dodson curve**, an inverted U-shaped relationship.

<LessonImage src="https://res.cloudinary.com/dkmutnahg/image/upload/v1778893904/graph.002_ayktx3.png" alt="Yerkes-Dodson curve" width={1366} height={432} />
[Image Source: Diamond D.M., et al. (2007)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1906714/)

With too little stress, arousal is low: you feel sluggish and unfocused. Under excessive stress, performance collapses because hormones overwhelm the prefrontal cortex. Peak performance sits in the middle: enough pressure to sharpen focus without triggering a panic response.

The optimal peak depends on the task. Simple physical tasks like running tolerate high arousal. Complex intellectual tasks that demand multi-step reasoning, such as a computer science or engineering exam, have a lower threshold because working memory degrades quickly under high stress.

A small amount of exam anxiety mobilizes energy and sharpens concentration. Problems arise when you interpret normal physical arousal as panic, creating a self-reinforcing stress loop.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Usual mistake: mistaking physical arousal for panic">
A racing heart, quickened breathing, and cold hands before an exam show that your sympathetic nervous system is delivering oxygen and glucose so your brain can work. If you tell yourself you are panicking, your brain generates more fear. If you remind yourself that your body is preparing to focus, performance stays high.
</Callout>

## Negativity Bias

The **negativity bias** is a cognitive pattern that amplifies exam stress.

The brain prioritizes mistakes and threats over neutral or positive events. In evolutionary history, missing a threat like a predator meant death, whereas missing an extra food source carried little cost. The amygdala flags threatening events with stress hormones, storing perceived failures quickly while routine successes leave little impression.

In an exam room, this bias distorts judgment. You might solve nineteen questions well and struggle with one. On the walk home, you replay that single missed question repeatedly, analyzing the error until you worry you failed the entire course. The nineteen successful answers leave little emotional weight because solving them felt routine.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Usual mistake: recalculating your grade on the walk home">
Calculating a worst-case score on the walk home based on the single problem you could not solve. Your brain downplays questions that went smoothly and fixates on the one that went wrong.
</Callout>

<Callout tone="mint" label="A practical check on post-exam doubt">
When you leave an exam convinced you failed, ask yourself: *Am I remembering everything, or only what went wrong?* Your brain will always make errors feel more prominent than successes, so factor in that bias before reaching a conclusion.
</Callout>

## "I Have Never Seen This Before"

This is where most exam marks are lost. You hit a question that looks unfamiliar, and your immediate reaction is shock.

What follows is often the wrong move: freezing in place, spending twenty minutes staring at a single problem, or deciding the exam is lost and rushing through the rest.

Exams in technical courses almost always include problems that most students cannot fully solve. Instructors use baseline questions to check core competence, intermediate problems to measure mastery, and stretch questions to separate top grades. Running into a problem you cannot solve right away is standard exam design, not an indicator that you are unprepared.

When you encounter an unfamiliar question, use this five-step triage protocol:

1. **Put down your pen for five seconds:** Stop writing immediately. A brief pause breaks the urge to scribble random equations.
2. **Identify the topic cluster (30 seconds):** Check your subject map. Determine which general topic this problem belongs to, and write relevant definitions, governing formulas, or given variables in the margin.
3. **Apply the 60-second rule:** If no viable solution path appears after one minute, stop working on it. Mark the question clearly in the margin with a circle or question mark.
4. **Turn the page immediately:** Move to the next problem. Avoid lingering on the stuck question.
5. **Return at the end:** Once you have banked the straightforward marks across the paper, return to the marked questions with your remaining time. Lower cortisol levels make problem-solving easier, and you can pick up partial credit by writing down known principles and logical steps.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Usual mistake: fighting a stuck problem on page one">
Wrestling with an unfamiliar question on page one for twenty minutes while straightforward marks sit unread at the back of the paper. You burn time and exhaust working memory on a low-probability question.
</Callout>

<Callout tone="mint" label="A practical reframe: stretch questions">
An unfamiliar question means the exam is doing its job of testing upper bounds. Your job is to secure points on the material you know, without letting one difficult problem steal time and attention from questions you can solve.
</Callout>

## What You Can Actually Do

**Before the exam:** The most effective defense against cortisol is disciplined preparation. Regular [retrieval practice](/courses/the-exam-mind/the-problem-with-how-you-study#how-memory-actually-works) builds genuine confidence grounded in evidence of what you know. Reliable sleep the night before protects working memory.

**In the exam room:** If you feel an acute spike of anxiety, activate the **parasympathetic nervous system**, the branch of the autonomic nervous system that acts as an internal brake, slowing heart rate and lowering blood pressure.

The fastest way to engage this brake is the **physiological sigh**, a two-breath inhalation pattern followed by a long exhale:

1. Take a continuous inhale through your nose for about two seconds.
2. Without exhaling, take a second, sharp sip of air through your nose to fully expand your lungs. This second inhale reinflates collapsed **alveoli**, the tiny air sacs where carbon dioxide collects during shallow breathing.
3. Exhale slowly and completely through your mouth for six to eight seconds.
4. Repeat this cycle two or three times.

The long exhalation increases pressure in your chest cavity, causing the heart to expand slightly. Sensory nerves detect that change and signal the brainstem through the vagus nerve to slow the heart rate. The exercise is silent, takes twenty seconds at your desk, and measurably lowers physical tension.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Usual mistake: rapid deep chest breathing">
Trying to calm down with rapid, forced chest breaths. Hyperventilating expels carbon dioxide too quickly, which constricts cerebral blood vessels, causes lightheadedness, and amplifies physical panic.
</Callout>

**After the exam:** Put a concrete number on your performance. Estimate how many marks you secured with reasonable confidence. That tally is almost always higher than the emotional reaction you feel right after leaving the room.
