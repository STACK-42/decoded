# The One-Week Plan

There is no perfect plan. I want to say that first, because the internet is full of color-coded weekly schedules that look very organized and have nothing to do with how exams feel when you are inside them.

The way we work, the time we have, almost everything varies from one person to the next, so pinning down a single schedule isn't wise. What I can give you are principles, things that hold regardless of your subject, your schedule, or how badly your preparation has gone so far. The plan you build has to be yours, but it should rest on something real.

## Before You Plan Anything: Build the Map

Before I touch any material, I build a **subject map**: a single-page overview of what a course is fundamentally about, how many main topics it has, how they connect, and roughly how much weight each one carries. I skip the details and the equations at this stage and look only at the shape of the subject.

When you sit down to study without this picture, your **working memory**, the small active workspace your brain uses to hold and connect ideas right now, has to do two jobs at once. Part of it tries to learn the formulas in front of you while the rest keeps guessing what those formulas are for and how much of the course is still left. That second job runs in the background the whole time and adds stress that has nothing to do with the content. Giving yourself a map frees that workspace and gives every new detail a place to attach.

Here is how to build a map step by step, using Numerical Analysis as an example:

1. **Gather the outline:** Put the syllabus, textbook table of contents, or lecture titles (say, 12 lectures) in front of you in one sitting, without studying any page in detail.
2. **Group lectures into 4 or 5 clusters on one sheet of paper:** For Numerical Analysis, those blocks might be *root finding*, *solving linear systems*, *interpolation*, *numerical integration*, and *differential equations*.
3. **Write the central problem at the top:** In one sentence, state what the whole course is trying to do. For Numerical Analysis, the whole subject is about *approximation*, finding values close enough to be useful when exact answers are impossible or too slow.
4. **Mark rough exam weight:** Beside each cluster, note how many lectures or past-exam questions belong to it so you can see where the bulk of the course sits.
5. **Run the Feynman test on each cluster:** Before opening a chapter, test whether you can state what each block does in plain words.

The **Feynman test** is simple: take the course's central idea and each main cluster, and explain them out loud in your own words as simply as possible, as if talking to someone who has never studied the subject. Whenever you catch yourself hiding behind vague words or repeating a slide title without knowing what it means, you have found the exact gap where you need to start.

<Callout tone="violet" label="Why the Feynman test works before studying">
A student of numerical analysis who memorizes five root-finding methods without realizing the whole course is about approximation will struggle with every unfamiliar problem. A student who explains the core idea out loud builds a framework that catches almost every formula underneath it.
</Callout>

<Callout tone="orange" label="Usual mistake: diving straight into page one">
Most students skip the map and start reading Lecture 1 right away because starting at page one feels like immediate progress. Three days later, they are buried in isolated derivations with no idea how the topics connect or how much material is left.
</Callout>

The map does not take long: an hour, maybe two if you are slow. I never skip it, and it saves far more time than that later.

## Now Plan the Week

Once you have the map, you can build a realistic week. Here are the four rules I plan from, followed by what they look like on a real calendar.

**Start with a topic checklist.** Turn each course's map into one written list of every topic you need to cover. A large share of finals stress comes from the vague feeling of not knowing how much work remains. Crossing topics off a visible list removes that uncertainty and lets you budget hours honestly.

**Follow the exam order and plan backwards from the exam dates.** The first exam on your calendar gets your first primary blocks of preparation time. Do not start with a later exam because you enjoy it more, because it feels easier, or because it intimidates you.

**Slice a huge exam that comes later.** If one subject is much larger than the others and its exam is third or fourth, you cannot wait until two days before it to start. Block 60 to 90 minutes for it across the earlier days while you prepare for the earlier exams. A big subject that gets focused blocks every other day for a week enters the final stretch in far better shape than one crammed into eight exhausted hours the night before, because [memory consolidation](/courses/the-exam-mind/the-problem-with-how-you-study#how-memory-actually-works) needs sleep between sessions.

**Prioritize every topic by two things: exam frequency and your weakness.** Score each checklist item on how often it appears in past exams and how shaky you are on it right now. A topic you already solve easily that rarely appears goes to the bottom of the queue. A topic you cannot explain cold that shows up every year goes straight to the top.

<Callout tone="violet" label="A practical shortcut: mining past exams">
Take the past two to five years of exams for a course and give them to an LLM, or tally them yourself. Ask it to group questions by topic and list which ones appear every year. Exams are written by people with habits, and those habits show up clearly across an archive.
</Callout>

Here is how those four rules work together across a concrete 7-day exam week with three courses: **Operating Systems (OS)** on Wednesday (Day 3), **Computer Networks** on Friday (Day 5), and a huge **Discrete Mathematics** exam on Sunday (Day 7).

1. **Sunday evening (planning, 45 minutes):** Turn the three maps into checklists. Tag each topic as *high* or *low* frequency from past papers, and *weak* or *solid* from a quick Feynman check.
2. **Monday and Tuesday (Days 1 to 2):** Put your primary blocks on Operating Systems (Exam 1), tackling its high-frequency weak topics first with retrieval practice. Add one 90-minute slice each afternoon on the hardest Discrete Mathematics topics, such as logic proofs or counting rules.
3. **Wednesday (Day 3):** Take the OS exam in the morning. Rest for an hour or two afterward, then run your first primary blocks on Computer Networks (Exam 2), plus one 90-minute Discrete Mathematics slice.
4. **Thursday (Day 4):** Primary focus on Computer Networks weak topics, plus one more 90-minute Discrete Mathematics slice. By Thursday night, Discrete Mathematics already has six hours across four separate days of sleep consolidation behind it.
5. **Friday (Day 5):** Take the Computer Networks exam, recover, then move full focus to Discrete Mathematics (Exam 3). Instead of facing an untouched mountain of chapters, half the hardest topics are already ready for recall.
6. **Saturday (Day 6):** Run timed retrieval practice on the remaining weak Discrete Mathematics topics during the day, switch to light review in the early evening, and stop in time for a full night of sleep.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Usual mistake: planning by comfort instead of the calendar">
Students often spend Day 1 on their favorite subject or the easiest chapters so they feel productive, or panic and pour three full days into the biggest exam at the end of the week while neglecting Exam 1. Plan from the exam dates backwards, and spend your energy on weak topics that show up often.
</Callout>

## The Environment Is Not a Detail

Some people structure their focus blocks with **Pomodoro**, a time-boxing method where you work with full focus for one fixed interval (traditionally 25 minutes), take a 5-minute break, and take a longer break after four rounds. I don't use it personally; I study in one-hour blocks and that works for me. What I am very firm about is taking the phone out of the room entirely. Turning it face down doesn't count if you still pick it up every five minutes.

The [research](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/691462) here is clear: just having a smartphone on the desk, even switched off or face down, reduces your available **cognitive capacity**, the limited pool of active mental bandwidth your brain has right now to reason, hold steps in working memory, and form memories.

Here is why a silent phone still costs you. Because your brain associates the phone with messages, feeds, and quick rewards, merely seeing it in your field of vision triggers an automatic impulse to check it. Holding yourself back from reaching for it takes active control from the same attention system you need for solving problems, so less bandwidth reaches the material.

There is a second cost whenever you do glance at a notification, called **attentional residue**: part of your mind stays attached to something else for minutes after you switch back to studying. Even a ten-second check leaves your brain half-occupied with the message you just read while you try to follow a proof.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Usual mistake: face-down on silent">
Putting your phone face down on silent next to your notebook feels disciplined, yet a phone within arm's reach still splits your attention and shrinks working memory. Put it in another room, and use website blockers on your laptop if you need them.
</Callout>

Protect the hours you actually sit down to work, because there are fewer of them than it feels like.

## What to Do the Night Before

Very little that is new.

The night before an exam, or the last few hours, are not for learning new material. I hate to break it to you, but your professor was right: that time is for light review, testing yourself gently on summaries you already built, and confirming what you know.

Trying to force brand-new chapters into memory the night before fails for two reasons. First, unfamiliar material crammed under late-night stress forms fragile, tangled traces that easily interfere with related concepts you already studied cleanly. Second, staying up until three in the morning to read the same chapter a fourth time steals the deep sleep your brain needs to lock in everything you already reviewed over the rest of the week. Trading two hours of sleep for one unfamiliar chapter hurts your recall across the whole exam.

Here is what a calm night before actually looks like:

1. **Early evening (45 to 60 minutes):** Run light retrieval practice on key formulas, definitions, or past mistakes you already covered earlier in the week.
2. **If you spot a topic you never studied:** Write down its name so you know it exists, accept that it is not worth sacrificing sleep over, and move on.
3. **One hour before bed:** Close your notes. Pack your bag, pens, ID, and calculator so the morning requires zero decisions.
4. **Sleep:** Go to bed at a normal hour.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Usual mistake: cramming a missed chapter at midnight">
Discovering an unstudied topic at 11 p.m. and deciding to master it before bed feels heroic, yet it trades away the sleep your brain needs to consolidate everything you already worked on all week.
</Callout>

What you actually need the night before is [sleep](/courses/the-exam-mind/exam-day#sleep-is-not-optional). That part is biology, and I think you know what it is like to sit an exam on no sleep.

<Callout tone="mint" label="The honest summary">
Build the map. Make the checklist. Study in the order the calendar demands, not the order you're comfortable with. Protect your focus hours. Sleep before the exam. None of this is revolutionary, but most people ignore at least two of these, and that is where the marks go.
</Callout>
