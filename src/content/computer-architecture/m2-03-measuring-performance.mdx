# Measuring Performance

Before you can make a computer faster, you must define what "faster" means. A common assumption is that a processor with a **clock rate** (or **clock speed**, the number of clock ticks per second) of 4.0 GHz must outperform one running at 2.5 GHz. In practice, clock rate alone tells you almost nothing about how quickly an application will complete.

The only definitive measure of computer performance is execution time. But execution time has two distinct meanings:

- **Wall-clock time** (or elapsed time): The total real-world time required to finish a task, including memory access stalls, disk and network I/O, and operating system multitasking overhead.
- **CPU execution time**: The time the processor actively spends executing machine instructions for that program, excluding time spent waiting for I/O or other programs.

## The iron law of processor performance

Three fundamental factors govern CPU execution time. Total execution time equals the total number of clock ticks multiplied by the duration of one tick:

$$
\text{CPU Execution Time} = \text{Clock Cycles} \times \text{Clock Period}
$$

Total clock cycles equal the number of instructions executed multiplied by the average cycles per instruction ($\text{Clock Cycles} = \text{Instruction Count} \times \text{CPI}$). Substituting gives the **CPU performance equation**:

$$
\text{CPU Execution Time} = \text{Instruction Count} \times \text{CPI} \times \text{Clock Period}
$$

Because the clock period $\tau$ is the reciprocal of the clock frequency ($f_{\text{clk}} = 1 / \tau$), the equation can also be written as:

$$
\text{CPU Execution Time} = \frac{\text{Instruction Count} \times \text{CPI}}{f_{\text{clk}}}
$$

Each factor reflects a distinct layer of the computer system:

1. **Instruction Count** ($IC$): The total number of machine instructions executed by the program. This is determined by the algorithm, the efficiency of the optimizing compiler, and the expressiveness of the [instruction set architecture](/courses/computer-architecture/instruction-formats).
2. **Cycles Per Instruction** ($\text{CPI}$): The average number of clock cycles required to execute one instruction. This is determined by the processor microarchitecture (pipelining depth, branch prediction, superscalar issue width) and the latency of the memory hierarchy. A **superscalar processor** contains multiple execution units, allowing it to issue and execute more than one instruction per clock cycle.
3. **Clock Cycle Time** ($\tau$ or $1 / f_{\text{clk}}$): The duration of a single clock tick in seconds (or ticks per second in gigahertz). This is determined by hardware datapath design, critical path propagation delay through logic gates, and silicon fabrication technology.

The inverse of CPI is **Instructions Per Cycle** ($\text{IPC} = 1 / \text{CPI}$). High-performance modern superscalar processors execute multiple instructions in parallel, achieving an IPC greater than 1.0 (or a CPI well below 1.0).

## The clock speed trap

Comparing processors solely by clock frequency is known as the **megahertz myth**.

<ClockTrap />

Consider Processor A running at 4.0 GHz with an average CPI of 2.0, and Processor B running at 2.5 GHz with an average CPI of 1.0. For a program of 10 billion instructions:

$$
\text{Time}_A = \frac{10 \times 10^9 \times 2.0}{4.0 \times 10^9} = 5.0\text{ seconds}
$$

$$
\text{Time}_B = \frac{10 \times 10^9 \times 1.0}{2.5 \times 10^9} = 4.0\text{ seconds}
$$

Processor B finishes a full second faster despite having a clock speed that is 37.5% lower.

## Working out weighted CPI

Different instruction classes require different numbers of clock cycles. A simple integer register addition may execute in 1 cycle, a conditional branch in 3 cycles, and a floating-point division in 20 cycles.

The overall CPI is the weighted average across all instruction types based on their execution frequency:

$$
\text{CPI} = \sum_{i=1}^n \left( \frac{IC_i}{IC} \times \text{CPI}_i \right) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (IC_i \times \text{CPI}_i)}{IC}
$$

where $IC_i$ is the number of times instruction class $i$ is executed, and $\text{CPI}_i$ is the cycle count for that class.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Trap: Averaging CPI directly without instruction weighting">
Taking a simple unweighted average of CPI values, $(CPI_1 + CPI_2 + \dots) / n$, produces incorrect results because instruction classes do not execute in equal proportions. An instruction class that accounts for 60% of all executions contributes six times as many cycles as one that accounts for only 10%. Always multiply each class CPI by its share of total instructions.
</Callout>

## MIPS and MFLOPS pitfalls

Two popular shorthand metrics attempt to capture processor speed in a single number:

**MIPS** (Million Instructions Per Second) measures the raw rate of instruction execution:

$$
\text{MIPS} = \frac{IC}{\text{CPU Time} \times 10^6} = \frac{f_{\text{clk}}}{\text{CPI} \times 10^6}
$$

Here is a comparison of two processors running at 200 MHz on a program divided into four instruction classes: ALU operations (1 cycle), conditional branches (2 to 3 cycles), memory loads and stores (4 cycles), and floating-point operations (5 cycles). For Machine A, the instruction frequencies are 38%, 15%, 42%, and 5%. For Machine B, the frequencies are 35%, 30%, 15%, and 20%:

<CpiCalculator />

The starting baseline numbers evaluate as follows:

$$
\text{CPI}_A = \frac{38(1) + 15(3) + 42(4) + 5(5)}{100} = 2.76 \qquad \text{MIPS}_A = \frac{200}{2.76} = 72.46
$$

$$
\text{CPI}_B = \frac{35(1) + 30(2) + 15(3) + 20(5)}{100} = 2.40 \qquad \text{MIPS}_B = \frac{200}{2.40} = 83.33
$$

While MIPS appears straightforward, comparing MIPS across different architectures is unreliable:

1. A [RISC processor](/courses/computer-architecture/risc-and-cisc) might execute 5 million simple instructions to accomplish a task at 500 MIPS.
2. A CISC processor might accomplish the exact same task using only 2 million complex instructions at 250 MIPS.
3. Both machines take the exact same wall-clock time, but the RISC processor posts twice the MIPS score.
4. If an inefficient compiler emits redundant instructions that do no useful work, the processor's MIPS score actually increases because CPI drops, even though total execution time worsens.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Trap: Treating MIPS as a universal benchmark across architectures">
MIPS measures the rate of instruction completion, not the rate of useful work accomplished. Because a RISC processor requires more instructions than a CISC processor to perform the same task, it can report a higher MIPS score while executing the workload in the exact same wall-clock time. In addition, an inefficient compiler that fails to remove redundant instructions can artificially inflate a CPU's MIPS score by running more trivial instructions per second.
</Callout>

For scientific, graphics, and machine learning workloads, **MFLOPS** (Million Floating-Point Operations Per Second) or **TFLOPS** ($10^{12}$ FLOPS) measures the rate of actual arithmetic operations (multiplications and additions) rather than raw machine instructions.

## Amdahl's law

When architects seek to speed up a computer, they often enhance a specific component, such as adding a dedicated vector unit or hardware floating-point multiplier.

Suppose a program takes $T_{\text{old}}$ to execute. An enhancement speeds up a fraction $f$ of the program's execution time by a factor of $s$. The remaining fraction $(1 - f)$ cannot use the enhancement and runs at normal speed.

The new execution time is:

$$
T_{\text{new}} = T_{\text{old}} \times (1 - f) + T_{\text{old}} \times \frac{f}{s} = T_{\text{old}} \left( (1 - f) + \frac{f}{s} \right)
$$

The overall speedup is the ratio of old execution time to new execution time:

$$
\text{Speedup}_{\text{overall}} = \frac{T_{\text{old}}}{T_{\text{new}}} = \frac{T_{\text{old}}}{T_{\text{old}} \left( (1 - f) + \dfrac{f}{s} \right)} = \frac{1}{(1 - f) + \dfrac{f}{s}}
$$

This formulation is **Amdahl's law**.

As the enhancement becomes infinitely fast ($s \to \infty$), the execution time of that component drops to zero ($\frac{f}{s} \to 0$), yielding an asymptotic upper bound:

$$
\text{Speedup}_{\max} = \frac{1}{1 - f}
$$

If a task spends 80% of its time on memory transfers ($f = 0.8$), making the CPU core infinitely fast ($s = \infty$) can yield at most:

$$
\text{Speedup}_{\max} = \frac{1}{1 - 0.8} = \frac{1}{0.2} = 5.0\times
$$

The remaining 80% of unaccelerated time sets a hard ceiling on overall system gains. This principle is why architects focus on the **common case**: optimizing what runs most frequently yields far greater returns than perfecting rare edge cases.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Trap: Applying Amdahl's law to code size rather than runtime">
The fraction $f$ in Amdahl's law is the fraction of total execution time, not the share of source code lines. An encryption loop that occupies only 2% of the codebase might consume 90% of execution time. Accelerating that 2% of code produces large speedups.
</Callout>

## Benchmarks and the geometric mean

To evaluate processors fairly, the computing industry relies on standardized benchmark suites such as **SPEC CPU** (Standard Performance Evaluation Corporation). These suites run real applications including compilers, chess engines, ray tracers, and fluid dynamics simulators.

When benchmark runtimes are normalized against a baseline reference machine, computing the standard arithmetic mean produces deceptive results that depend on which machine is chosen as the baseline.

Consider two computers running two benchmark programs:
- Program 1: Computer A takes 1 second; Computer B takes 10 seconds.
- Program 2: Computer A takes 100 seconds; Computer B takes 10 seconds.

If you normalize runtimes against Computer A:
- Program 1 ratio: Computer A = 1.0, Computer B = 10.0.
- Program 2 ratio: Computer A = 1.0, Computer B = 0.1.
- Arithmetic mean: Computer A = 1.0, Computer B = $(10.0 + 0.1) / 2 = 5.05$. Computer A appears 5 times faster than Computer B.

If you normalize runtimes against Computer B:
- Program 1 ratio: Computer A = 0.1, Computer B = 1.0.
- Program 2 ratio: Computer A = 10.0, Computer B = 1.0.
- Arithmetic mean: Computer A = $(0.1 + 10.0) / 2 = 5.05$, Computer B = 1.0. Computer B now appears 5 times faster than Computer A.

The conclusion reverses depending entirely on the choice of baseline machine. To preserve consistent relative rankings across any baseline, architects use the **geometric mean**:

$$
\text{Geometric Mean} = \sqrt[n]{\prod_{i=1}^n \text{Execution Time Ratio}_i}
$$

For the benchmark above:
- Normalized to Computer A: Geometric Mean(B) = $\sqrt{10.0 \times 0.1} = \sqrt{1.0} = 1.0$.
- Normalized to Computer B: Geometric Mean(A) = $\sqrt{0.1 \times 10.0} = \sqrt{1.0} = 1.0$.

The geometric mean guarantees that the performance ratio between any two computers remains identical regardless of which computer is chosen as the baseline.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Trap: Assuming a compiler that reduces CPI always improves speed">
A compiler might replace a 4-cycle multiplication instruction with six 1-cycle additions and bit shifts. The average CPI drops from 4.0 to 1.0. However, the total instruction count increases from 1 to 6. The original code took $1 \times 4 = 4$ clock cycles, while the new code takes $6 \times 1 = 6$ clock cycles. Total execution time increased by 50% despite the lower CPI score.
</Callout>

<ModelAnswer title="Problem 1: Evaluating compiler optimization tradeoffs">
A software benchmark executes on a 2.0 GHz processor with the following instruction distribution and cycle costs:

| Instruction class | Instruction count ($IC_i$) | Clock cycles per instruction ($\text{CPI}_i$) |
|---|---|---|
| Arithmetic / Logic | $60 \times 10^6$ | 1 |
| Data Transfer (Load/Store) | $30 \times 10^6$ | 2 |
| Branch | $10 \times 10^6$ | 2 |

An optimizing compiler is applied to the codebase. It eliminates 20% of the arithmetic instructions and 10% of the data transfer instructions without changing branch instructions. However, scheduling changes increase the average CPI of branches to 3 cycles.

1. Calculate the initial average CPI and execution time before optimization.
2. Calculate the new average CPI and execution time after optimization.
3. Determine the overall speedup achieved by the compiler.

---

**Solution:**

**Part 1: Initial performance:**
Total initial instruction count:
$$IC_{\text{initial}} = (60 + 30 + 10) \times 10^6 = 100 \times 10^6\text{ instructions}$$

Total initial clock cycles:
$$\text{Cycles}_{\text{initial}} = (60 \times 1) + (30 \times 2) + (10 \times 2) = 60 + 60 + 20 = 140 \times 10^6\text{ cycles}$$

Initial average CPI:
$$\text{CPI}_{\text{initial}} = \frac{140 \times 10^6}{100 \times 10^6} = 1.40$$

Initial CPU execution time at 2.0 GHz ($2.0 \times 10^9$ cycles/second):
$$\text{Time}_{\text{initial}} = \frac{140 \times 10^6}{2.0 \times 10^9} = 0.070\text{ seconds} = 70.0\text{ ms}$$

**Part 2: Performance after optimization:**
New instruction counts:
- Arithmetic: $60 \times 10^6 \times (1 - 0.20) = 48 \times 10^6$
- Data Transfer: $30 \times 10^6 \times (1 - 0.10) = 27 \times 10^6$
- Branch: $10 \times 10^6$ (unchanged)

New total instruction count:
$$IC_{\text{new}} = (48 + 27 + 10) \times 10^6 = 85 \times 10^6\text{ instructions}$$

New total clock cycles:
$$\text{Cycles}_{\text{new}} = (48 \times 1) + (27 \times 2) + (10 \times 3) = 48 + 54 + 30 = 132 \times 10^6\text{ cycles}$$

New average CPI:
$$\text{CPI}_{\text{new}} = \frac{132 \times 10^6}{85 \times 10^6} \approx 1.553$$

New CPU execution time:
$$\text{Time}_{\text{new}} = \frac{132 \times 10^6}{2.0 \times 10^9} = 0.066\text{ seconds} = 66.0\text{ ms}$$

**Part 3: Overall speedup:**
$$\text{Speedup} = \frac{\text{Time}_{\text{initial}}}{\text{Time}_{\text{new}}} = \frac{0.070}{0.066} \approx 1.061\times$$

The optimized program executes 6.1% faster. Average CPI worsened from 1.40 to 1.55, but overall execution time improved because the total instruction count dropped from 100 million to 85 million.
</ModelAnswer>

<ModelAnswer title="Problem 2: Amdahl's law hardware design target">
A web server spends 60% of its execution time processing database queries and 40% running web server logic.

Engineering proposes adding a dedicated hardware coprocessor that accelerates database queries by a factor of $k$.

1. What overall speedup is achieved if the coprocessor accelerates database queries by $k = 4$?
2. What is the maximum theoretical speedup possible if database query execution time is reduced to zero?
3. What query speedup factor $k$ is required to achieve an overall system speedup of exactly $2.0\times$?

---

**Solution:**

**Part 1: Overall speedup for $k = 4$:**
Here, $f = 0.60$ and $s = 4$.
Applying Amdahl's law:
$$\text{Speedup} = \frac{1}{(1 - 0.60) + \dfrac{0.60}{4}} = \frac{1}{0.40 + 0.15} = \frac{1}{0.55} \approx 1.818\times$$

**Part 2: Maximum theoretical speedup ($k \to \infty$):**
$$\text{Speedup}_{\max} = \frac{1}{1 - f} = \frac{1}{1 - 0.60} = \frac{1}{0.40} = 2.50\times$$

Even if the database coprocessor runs infinitely fast, the overall web server cannot exceed a $2.5\times$ speedup because the remaining 40% of unaccelerated logic sets a baseline bound.

**Part 3: Required speedup $k$ for overall $2.0\times$ speedup:**
Set $\text{Speedup} = 2.0$:
$$2.0 = \frac{1}{(1 - 0.60) + \dfrac{0.60}{k}}$$
$$2.0 \times \left( 0.40 + \frac{0.60}{k} \right) = 1.0$$
$$0.80 + \frac{1.20}{k} = 1.0$$
$$\frac{1.20}{k} = 0.20 \implies k = \frac{1.20}{0.20} = 6$$

The database query coprocessor must achieve a $6\times$ speedup to deliver a $2.0\times$ overall system performance gain.
</ModelAnswer>

<ModelAnswer title="Problem 3: Processor comparison across architectures using the Iron Law">
Two processors, Machine A and Machine B, implement different instruction set architectures. A standard computational benchmark is compiled for each machine:

- **Machine A:** Clock rate $f_A = 4.0\text{ GHz}$, Instruction count $IC_A = 1.2 \times 10^9$, average $\text{CPI}_A = 1.8$.
- **Machine B:** Clock rate $f_B = 2.5\text{ GHz}$, Instruction count $IC_B = 0.6 \times 10^9$, average $\text{CPI}_B = 1.2$.

1. Calculate the CPU execution time for both machines.
2. Determine which machine is faster, and calculate the speedup factor.
3. Compute the MIPS rating for both machines, and explain why the machine with the higher MIPS score is actually the slower computer.

---

**Solution:**

**Part 1: CPU execution time:**
Using the Iron Law ($\text{CPU Time} = \dfrac{IC \times \text{CPI}}{f}$):
- Machine A:
  $$\text{Time}_A = \frac{1.2 \times 10^9 \times 1.8}{4.0 \times 10^9\text{ Hz}} = \frac{2.16 \times 10^9}{4.0 \times 10^9} = 0.540\text{ seconds}$$
- Machine B:
  $$\text{Time}_B = \frac{0.6 \times 10^9 \times 1.2}{2.5 \times 10^9\text{ Hz}} = \frac{0.72 \times 10^9}{2.5 \times 10^9} = 0.288\text{ seconds}$$

**Part 2: Performance comparison:**
Machine B is faster because its execution time is substantially lower:
$$\text{Speedup} = \frac{\text{Time}_A}{\text{Time}_B} = \frac{0.540}{0.288} = 1.875\times$$
Machine B delivers an 87.5% speedup over Machine A.

**Part 3: MIPS comparison and pitfall analysis:**
$$\text{MIPS} = \frac{f}{\text{CPI} \times 10^6}$$
- Machine A:
  $$\text{MIPS}_A = \frac{4.0 \times 10^9}{1.8 \times 10^6} \approx 2{,}222.2\text{ MIPS}$$
- Machine B:
  $$\text{MIPS}_B = \frac{2.5 \times 10^9}{1.2 \times 10^6} \approx 2{,}083.3\text{ MIPS}$$

Machine A executes 2,222 million instructions per second, while Machine B executes only 2,083 million instructions per second. Yet Machine A takes almost twice as long to finish the program!
Machine A requires twice as many total instructions ($1.2 \times 10^9$ vs $0.6 \times 10^9$) to perform the exact same computation. MIPS measures instruction throughput rather than work accomplished. Because different ISAs express the same high-level task with different numbers of instructions, comparing MIPS across architectures is fundamentally flawed.
</ModelAnswer>

<ModelAnswer title="Problem 4: Multicore scaling under Amdahl's law with serial fraction">
A scientific simulation program spends 15% of its execution time in non-parallelizable serial code (file initialization, data reading, and barrier synchronization). The remaining 85% of execution time can be divided evenly across any number of parallel processor cores ($P$).

1. Calculate the overall system speedup when executed on $P = 4, 8, 16,$ and $64$ processor cores.
2. Determine the asymptotic theoretical limit of speedup as the core count approaches infinity ($P \to \infty$).
3. If an engineering specification requires achieving an overall speedup of at least $5.0\times$, determine whether this goal is mathematically achievable, and if so, find the minimum integer core count $P$ required.

---

**Solution:**

**Part 1: Speedup across core counts:**
Applying Amdahl's law with serial fraction $f_{\text{serial}} = 0.15$ and parallel fraction $f_{\text{parallel}} = 0.85$:
$$\text{Speedup}(P) = \frac{1}{0.15 + \dfrac{0.85}{P}}$$

- For $P = 4$:
  $$\text{Speedup}(4) = \frac{1}{0.15 + \dfrac{0.85}{4}} = \frac{1}{0.15 + 0.2125} = \frac{1}{0.3625} \approx 2.759\times$$
- For $P = 8$:
  $$\text{Speedup}(8) = \frac{1}{0.15 + \dfrac{0.85}{8}} = \frac{1}{0.15 + 0.10625} = \frac{1}{0.25625} \approx 3.902\times$$
- For $P = 16$:
  $$\text{Speedup}(16) = \frac{1}{0.15 + \dfrac{0.85}{16}} = \frac{1}{0.15 + 0.053125} = \frac{1}{0.203125} \approx 4.923\times$$
- For $P = 64$:
  $$\text{Speedup}(64) = \frac{1}{0.15 + \dfrac{0.85}{64}} = \frac{1}{0.15 + 0.01328} = \frac{1}{0.16328} \approx 6.124\times$$

**Part 2: Asymptotic speedup bound ($P \to \infty$):**
$$\text{Speedup}_{\max} = \lim_{P \to \infty} \frac{1}{0.15 + \dfrac{0.85}{P}} = \frac{1}{0.15} \approx 6.667\times$$
Regardless of how many thousands of cores are added, the speedup can never exceed $6.67\times$.

**Part 3: Minimum cores for $5.0\times$ speedup:**
Because $5.0 < 6.667$, the target is achievable:
$$5.0 = \frac{1}{0.15 + \dfrac{0.85}{P}}$$
$$0.15 + \frac{0.85}{P} = \frac{1}{5.0} = 0.20$$
$$\frac{0.85}{P} = 0.20 - 0.15 = 0.05$$
$$P = \frac{0.85}{0.05} = 17 \text{ cores}$$

The system requires at least 17 dedicated parallel cores to deliver a $5.0\times$ speedup.
</ModelAnswer>

<ModelAnswer title="Problem 5: Memory stall CPI and memory hierarchy impact">
A processor has an ideal base cycle cost $\text{CPI}_{\text{base}} = 1.2$ when all memory accesses hit in the L1 cache. The application workload consists of 30% data memory reference instructions (loads and stores).
The cache memory hierarchy operates with the following specifications:
- L1 data cache miss rate: 4%.
- L1 miss penalty (time to service from L2 cache): 10 clock cycles.
- L2 cache local miss rate: 20%.
- L2 miss penalty (additional time to service from main memory DRAM): 100 clock cycles (total 110 cycles when missing both caches).

1. Calculate the memory stall cycles per instruction contributed by the cache hierarchy.
2. Calculate the effective average CPI ($\text{CPI}_{\text{eff}} = \text{CPI}_{\text{base}} + \text{Memory Stalls}$).
3. Calculate what percentage of total execution time is spent stalled waiting on memory.

---

**Solution:**

**Part 1: Memory stall cycles per instruction:**
Memory accesses occur on 30% of instructions. When an access misses in L1 (4% of accesses), it incurs a 10-cycle penalty to check L2. Furthermore, 20% of those L1 misses also miss in L2, incurring the 10-cycle L2 check plus 100 cycles to fetch the block from DRAM ($10 + 100 = 110$ cycles):
$$\text{Stall cycles}_{\text{L1 miss, L2 hit}} = 0.04 \times (1 - 0.20) \times 10 = 0.032 \times 10 = 0.32\text{ cycles/access}$$
$$\text{Stall cycles}_{\text{L2 miss}} = 0.04 \times 0.20 \times (10 + 100) = 0.008 \times 110 = 0.88\text{ cycles/access}$$
$$\text{Total stalls per memory access} = 0.32 + 0.88 = 1.20\text{ cycles/access}$$

Factoring in the 30% memory reference frequency:
$$\text{Memory Stalls} = 0.30 \times 1.20 = 0.36\text{ cycles per instruction}$$

**Part 2: Effective overall CPI:**
$$\text{CPI}_{\text{eff}} = \text{CPI}_{\text{base}} + \text{Memory Stalls} = 1.20 + 0.36 = 1.56\text{ cycles per instruction}$$

**Part 3: Percentage of execution time spent stalled:**
$$\text{Fraction stalled} = \frac{\text{Memory Stalls}}{\text{CPI}_{\text{eff}}} = \frac{0.36}{1.56} \approx 0.2308 \implies 23.08\text{\%}$$
The CPU core spends nearly a quarter of its execution time waiting for memory transfers, demonstrating why cache hierarchy tuning is as vital to processor performance as ALU clock speed.
</ModelAnswer>

<ModelAnswer title="Problem 6: SPEC benchmark geometric mean vs arithmetic mean">
A benchmark suite evaluates two computers against a standard Reference Machine $R$ across three programs:

| Program | Reference Machine $R$ | Computer $A$ | Computer $B$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Program 1 ($P_1$) | 100 s | 20 s | 50 s |
| Program 2 ($P_2$) | 200 s | 100 s | 50 s |
| Program 3 ($P_3$) | 500 s | 100 s | 250 s |

1. Calculate the SPEC performance ratio for each program on Computer $A$ and Computer $B$ relative to Reference Machine $R$ (where $\text{Ratio} = \text{Time}_{\text{Ref}} / \text{Time}_{\text{Test}}$).
2. Compute the geometric mean of the SPEC ratios for both computers. Which computer is faster overall?
3. Explain why the arithmetic mean of normalized execution ratios is mathematically flawed when evaluating computer performance.

---

**Solution:**

**Part 1: Individual SPEC ratios relative to Reference $R$:**
- Computer $A$:
  - $P_1$: $100 / 20 = 5.00$
  - $P_2$: $200 / 100 = 2.00$
  - $P_3$: $500 / 100 = 5.00$
- Computer $B$:
  - $P_1$: $100 / 50 = 2.00$
  - $P_2$: $200 / 50 = 4.00$
  - $P_3$: $500 / 250 = 2.00$

**Part 2: Geometric mean computation:**
$$\text{GM} = \sqrt[n]{\prod_{i=1}^n r_i}$$
- Computer $A$:
  $$\text{GM}_A = \sqrt[3]{5.00 \times 2.00 \times 5.00} = \sqrt[3]{50.0} \approx 3.684$$
- Computer $B$:
  $$\text{GM}_B = \sqrt[3]{2.00 \times 4.00 \times 2.00} = \sqrt[3]{16.0} \approx 2.520$$

Computer $A$ is faster overall by a factor of:
$$\frac{\text{GM}_A}{\text{GM}_B} = \frac{3.684}{2.520} \approx 1.462\times \text{ (46.2\% faster)}$$

**Part 3: Flaw of arithmetic mean for normalized metrics:**
If one used the arithmetic mean of ratios, the result would depend arbitrarily on which machine is chosen as the baseline. If Computer $A$ were chosen as the reference, normalized ratios for Computer $B$ would be $20/50 = 0.4$, $100/50 = 2.0$, and $100/250 = 0.4$, giving an arithmetic mean of $(0.4 + 2.0 + 0.4)/3 = 0.933 < 1.0$, suggesting Computer $B$ is faster, while relative to $R$ the arithmetic mean for $A$ is $(5+2+5)/3 = 4.0$ vs $(2+4+2)/3 = 2.67$ for $B$, suggesting $A$ is faster.
Only the geometric mean satisfies the property of consistency: $\text{GM}(A/B) = \text{GM}(A/R) / \text{GM}(B/R)$, ensuring that the ranking of computers remains invariant regardless of the chosen reference machine.
</ModelAnswer>
