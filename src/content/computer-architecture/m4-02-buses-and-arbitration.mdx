# Buses and Arbitration

Inside a computer, many hardware components must exchange data. Connecting every component to every other component with dedicated point-to-point wires would require millions of individual copper traces, consuming excessive board space and motherboard layers. Instead, components share common communication pathways called **buses**. Because a bus is a shared medium, devices require clear electrical conventions and arbitration protocols so signals do not collide.

## What is a bus?

A **bus** is a shared communication pathway consisting of parallel conductors that connect two or more hardware devices. A single conductive wire within a bus is a **bus line**, carrying binary voltage signals representing $0$ or $1$.

A complete system bus comprises three distinct functional signal groups:

| Signal Group | Representative Lines | Functional Purpose | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Data Lines** | $D_0 \dots D_{63}$ | Carries instructions, operands, and I/O data words | Bidirectional |
| **Address Lines** | $A_0 \dots A_{31}$ or $A_{47}$ | Selects the target memory address or I/O device port | Driven by Master |
| **Control Lines** | $\overline{\text{MEMR}}$, $\overline{\text{MEMW}}$, $\overline{\text{IOR}}$, $\overline{\text{IOW}}$, `CLK`, `READY` | Coordinates timing, commands, bus requests, and interrupts | Unidirectional or Shared |

The number of data lines is the **bus width**, which determines how many bytes move in a single transfer cycle. An $n$-line address bus selects among $2^n$ distinct memory bytes or I/O ports. A 32-bit address bus addresses $4\text{ GB}$ ($2^{32}$ bytes) of space, while a 48-bit bus addresses $256\text{ TB}$ ($2^{48}$ bytes).

A single complete sequence of bus operations required to read or write a data item is a **bus transaction**.

In any bus transaction, the participating devices take on one of two roles:

- A **bus master**: An active hardware device (such as the CPU, a DMA controller, or a high-speed network controller) that initiates transfers by driving address lines and issuing control strobes.
- A **bus slave**: A passive hardware component (such as a DRAM module or a basic peripheral status port) that monitors the bus, decodes its address, and only responds to commands asserted by a master.

### The electrical problem of shared lines

When multiple semiconductor chips connect to the same metal wire, two chips driving conflicting voltages at the same time (one driving $3.3\text{ V}$ and another driving $0\text{ V}$) create a low-resistance short circuit. This causes **bus contention**, drawing high electrical currents and corrupting data.

To prevent this, every device connects to shared bus lines through a [tri-state buffer](/courses/logic-design/memory-unit#tri-state-logic-and-shared-system-buses). A tri-state buffer provides three distinct electrical output states: logic 1 (high voltage), logic 0 (ground), and **high impedance** (**Hi-Z**).

In the Hi-Z state, the device's output pins act as an electrical open circuit, completely disconnecting the chip from the copper wire. Only the single currently granted bus master is permitted to drive voltages onto the wires; all other devices keep their bus drivers in the Hi-Z state.

## The physical limits of wide parallel buses

Early computers routed all components through a single, wide parallel backplane bus (such as ISA, VME, or early PCI). As microprocessors accelerated from megahertz to gigahertz frequencies, shared parallel buses ran into physical barriers:

1. **Capacitive Loading**: Every device pin and solder pad attached to a multi-drop bus adds parasitic capacitance. This capacitance increases the $RC$ time constant of the trace, rounding off sharp digital voltage transitions and capping maximum clock frequencies.
2. **Clock Skew**: In a 64-bit parallel bus, minute variations in copper trace lengths, board dielectric thickness, and solder joints mean that 64 bits transmitted simultaneously arrive at the receiver at slightly different times. When clock periods drop below a few nanoseconds, this **clock skew** exceeds the timing window, corrupting the received word.
3. **Electromagnetic Crosstalk**: As clock frequencies rise into hundreds of megahertz, rapid current transitions on one parallel wire radiate electromagnetic fields that induce noise voltages on adjacent wires.
4. **Trace Routing Density**: Routing 64 or 128 matched-length parallel copper traces across multiple motherboard layers consumes board area and creates manufacturing bottlenecks.

| Physical Characteristic | Wide Parallel Bus (e.g., PCI) | Switched Serial Interconnect (e.g., PCIe) |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical Topology** | Shared multi-drop copper bus | Point-to-point dedicated links |
| **Clock Distribution** | Separate clock line; high clock skew | Clock embedded in data stream (zero skew) |
| **Signaling Method** | Single-ended voltage relative to ground | Differential signaling pair ($\text{Tx}^+/\text{Tx}^-$) |
| **Bandwidth Scaling** | Add more parallel wires (worsens skew) | Add more lanes ($\times 1, \times 4, \times 8, \times 16$) |
| **Maximum Frequency** | Capped at $33\text{ MHz}$ to $66\text{ MHz}$ | Operates at $2.5\text{ GHz}$ to $32\text{ GHz}$ per lane |

<Callout tone="orange" label="Trap: Wider parallel buses cannot always run faster">
Doubling bus width from 32 bits to 64 bits does not double data throughput in practice. Widening a parallel bus increases capacitive load and worsens clock skew between the outermost and innermost traces. Designers often have to reduce the bus clock frequency, cutting into the throughput gain.
</Callout>

### The shift to switched serial fabrics (PCIe)

Modern computer architectures resolved these physical limits by replacing shared parallel buses with high-speed, point-to-point **switched serial fabrics**, exemplified by PCI Express (PCIe):

- **Differential Signaling**: Instead of measuring a single voltage relative to a noisy motherboard ground, PCIe transmits each signal across a pair of complementary wires ($\text{Tx}^+$ and $\text{Tx}^-$). The receiver measures the voltage difference $(V^+ - V^-)$. External electromagnetic interference affects both wires identically, so the difference remains unaffected, canceling noise.
- **Embedded Clocking and Line Coding**: PCIe eliminates the separate clock wire entirely. The transmitter uses **line coding** (such as 8b/10b in PCIe 1.0/2.0 or 128b/130b in PCIe 3.0+) to guarantee frequent voltage transitions in the data stream. The receiver uses an internal Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) to extract the clock directly from the data bitstream, eliminating clock skew completely.
- **Lanes and Aggregation**: A single PCIe **lane** consists of two differential pairs (four wires total: transmit $\text{Tx}^+/\text{Tx}^-$ and receive $\text{Rx}^+/\text{Rx}^-$). Bandwidth scales linearly by aggregating lanes into links: $\times 1, \times 4, \times 8$, or $\times 16$.
- **Packetized Switching**: Devices do not share physical wires. A central PCIe switch routes packetized messages directly between source and destination endpoints, enabling multiple devices to communicate simultaneously without bus contention.

### The modern interconnect hierarchy

Today's computers organize communication in a hierarchy of specialized links connected by bridges:

- **Processor-Memory Interconnect**: Short, point-to-point coherent interconnects (such as Intel UPI/QPI or AMD Infinity Fabric) operating at gigahertz frequencies directly between CPU sockets and memory controllers.
- **System Expansion Fabric**: PCIe links connecting graphics processors, NVMe SSDs, and high-speed network interfaces to the CPU Root Complex (the silicon bridge built into the processor die that joins the CPU cores and memory to the PCIe fabric).
- **Peripheral Buses**: Slower serial buses (USB, SATA, SPI, I2C, UART) managed through a chipset (Platform Controller Hub, or PCH).

## Bus protocols and timing: synchronous vs. asynchronous

The rules governing when an address is valid, when data is sampled, and how devices coordinate transfers form the **bus protocol**.

### Synchronous bus timing

In a **synchronous bus**, every bus event is coordinated by the edges of a shared system clock. All devices on the bus must run at the same clock frequency or an integer divisor of it.

| Clock Cycle | Transaction Phase | Master Actions | Slave Actions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cycle T1** | **Address Phase** | Drives target address onto address lines; asserts Read strobe | Decodes address lines; checks chip-select logic |
| **Cycle T2** | **Access / Wait Phase** | Holds address and command lines steady; checks `READY` line | Accesses memory cell or internal register; holds `WAIT` low if slow |
| **Cycle T3** | **Data Phase** | Samples data bus on rising clock edge; latches data; releases bus | Drives requested data onto data bus; asserts `READY` high |

If a slave device (such as slow DRAM) cannot retrieve data within a single clock cycle, it asserts a `WAIT` signal (or pulls `READY` low). The CPU senses this signal and inserts one or more **wait states** (idle clock cycles where the bus master holds all address and control signals constant), waiting for the slave to catch up.

Synchronous buses require minimal control logic and achieve high throughput over short physical distances. However, they suffer from clock skew over long wires, and every transaction must run at the speed of the slowest participating device unless wait states are inserted.

### Asynchronous bus timing

In an **asynchronous bus**, there is no shared bus clock. Timing is controlled entirely by interlocking control signals using a four-phase [handshake](/courses/computer-architecture/io-methods#asynchronous-handshaking-protocols):

1. The bus master asserts the memory address and read control lines.
2. The master asserts `Master Sync` (`MSYN`, or Address Strobe), signaling that the address lines are valid and settled.
3. The slave device decodes the address, accesses the requested data, drives the data bus, and asserts `Slave Sync` (`SSYN`, or Data Acknowledge).
4. The master detects `SSYN`, captures the data into its internal registers, and deasserts `MSYN`.
5. The slave detects the release of `MSYN`, clears its data lines to Hi-Z, and deasserts `SSYN`.

Asynchronous buses accommodate devices of wildly different speeds without wasting cycles, and they are immune to clock skew over long cables. However, the round-trip signal propagation delay and handshake edge detection add protocol overhead to every single transfer.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Trap: Bus clock rate does not equal payload throughput">
A synchronous bus operating at a high clock rate (such as $100\text{ MHz}$) does not necessarily deliver high payload data throughput. If a memory transfer requires 1 cycle for addressing, 3 wait states for DRAM access, and 1 cycle for data transfer (5 cycles total for one 4-byte word), the bus is actively transferring payload data during only $1/5 = 20\%$ of its operational cycles.
</Callout>

## Arbitration: whose turn is it?

In any multi-device computer, multiple components act as a **bus master**: an active unit capable of initiating data transfers. The CPU, multiple DMA controllers, graphics processors, and network cards can all demand bus access simultaneously.

If two bus masters drive the address or data lines at the same time, the signals collide, producing corrupt data and electrical faults. **Bus arbitration** is the hardware mechanism that decides which bus master gains control of the bus when multiple requests collide.

<BusArbiter />

An arbitration scheme balances three primary design goals:

1. **Priority**: Time-critical devices (like high-speed network interfaces or real-time video capture controllers with small buffers) must receive immediate bus access to prevent buffer overflow.
2. **Fairness**: Low-priority devices (such as background CPU processing) must not be starved of bus access indefinitely. **Starvation** is the condition where a master never receives bus access because higher-priority devices monopolize the bus.
3. **Hardware Overhead**: The arbitration protocol should require minimal dedicated wires and simple silicon logic.

### 1. Daisy chaining (serial grant)

In a **daisy chain** arbitration protocol, all bus masters share a single Bus Request (`BR`) line and a single Bus Busy (`BBUSY`) line. A single Bus Grant (`BG`) line leaves the central arbiter and loops serially through each device in physical order.

| Signal Line | Topology | Functional Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Bus Request (`BR`)** | Common shared line (wired-OR) | Pulled active by any master that needs bus access |
| **Bus Busy (`BBUSY`)** | Common shared line (wired-OR) | Held active by the current bus master while executing a transfer |
| **Bus Grant (`BG`)** | Serial daisy-chained wire | Leaves arbiter, passes from Master $i$ output to Master $i+1$ input |

- When one or more devices need the bus, they pull the common `BR` line active.
- The arbiter senses `BR` and asserts the `BG` signal.
- The `BG` signal enters Master 0 (the device physically closest to the arbiter).
- If Master 0 requested the bus, it intercepts the grant, refuses to pass `BG` down the chain, asserts `BBUSY`, and takes control of the bus.
- If Master 0 did not request the bus, it passes `BG` out to Master 1.
- The grant signal continues snaking through devices until it reaches the first requesting master.

**Trade-offs:** Daisy chaining uses only three control wires regardless of how many devices are attached, and the central arbiter is simple. However, priority is fixed by physical slot position: Master 0 always wins. If Master 0 generates continuous requests, all downstream devices suffer from starvation. In addition, grant propagation delay increases linearly with the number of devices ($O(N)$), and if any device in the chain fails or is removed, the grant signal breaks for all downstream masters.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Trap: Daisy chaining is not fair">
All devices share the same grant wire, but they do not receive equal turns. Physical position completely dictates priority. The master closest to the arbiter intercepts the grant first. If Master 0 has a continuous stream of transfers, downstream masters and the CPU receive zero bus time, causing starvation.
</Callout>

### 2. Centralized parallel arbitration

In **centralized parallel arbitration**, each individual bus master has dedicated, independent Bus Request ($\text{REQ}_i$) and Bus Grant ($\text{GNT}_i$) lines connected directly to a central arbiter chip.

| Master | Request Line | Grant Line | Connection to Central Arbiter |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Master 0 (e.g., GPU)** | $\text{REQ}_0$ | $\text{GNT}_0$ | Dedicated point-to-point trace pair |
| **Master 1 (e.g., NVMe SSD)** | $\text{REQ}_1$ | $\text{GNT}_1$ | Dedicated point-to-point trace pair |
| **Master 2 (e.g., Network)** | $\text{REQ}_2$ | $\text{GNT}_2$ | Dedicated point-to-point trace pair |
| **Master 3 (e.g., CPU)** | $\text{REQ}_3$ | $\text{GNT}_3$ | Dedicated point-to-point trace pair |

Because the arbiter sees every active request simultaneously, it can execute sophisticated prioritization algorithms:

- **Fixed Priority**: Requests pass through an internal [priority encoder](/courses/logic-design/comparators-decoders-encoders#priority-encoders) (e.g., Master 0 > Master 1 > Master 2). Real-time devices are guaranteed minimal latency, but low-priority masters can still starve under heavy loads.
- **Round-Robin (Rotating Priority)**: The arbiter maintains an internal pointer to the most recently served master, $k$. In the next arbitration cycle, priority begins at $(k + 1) \bmod N$. This guarantees fairness: no device can be starved, and maximum waiting time is bounded to $(N - 1)$ transactions.
- **Dynamic Priority**: The operating system or hardware controller dynamically changes master priority levels by writing to configuration registers inside the arbiter.

Parallel arbitration completes decisions in a single clock cycle and isolates device failures. Its drawback is line overhead: connecting $N$ masters requires $2N$ dedicated control lines.

### 3. Distributed arbitration

**Distributed arbitration** eliminates the central arbiter chip entirely. All bus masters participate in the arbitration decision using shared control and priority identification lines.

A classic implementation is **wired-OR self-selection** (used in SCSI and CAN bus architectures):

- Each bus master is assigned a unique $k$-bit binary priority ID.
- The arbitration lines use an **open-collector** (or open-drain) configuration with a **pull-up resistor**. Any device can pull a line to ground (logic 0), but when no device drives the line, the resistor pulls it to logic 1. Thus, a single 0 driven by any device overrides a 1.
- When arbitration begins, each requesting master asserts its priority ID on the shared arbitration bus, bit by bit from most significant bit to least significant bit.
- If Master A drives a 0 onto line $j$, but detects that line $j$ is currently held at 1 by another device, Master A realizes that a higher-priority master is also competing.
- Master A immediately withdraws its lower-order priority bits and concedes the bus.
- The highest-priority master sees its full ID intact on the bus lines and claims ownership.

Distributed arbitration provides high reliability with zero single point of failure, but it requires arbitration logic in every device interface.

## Split-transaction protocols

In a traditional synchronous or asynchronous bus transaction, a memory read holds the bus locked for the entire duration of the transfer:

$$\text{Total Bus Lock Time} = T_{\text{address}} + T_{\text{memory\_access\_latency}} + T_{\text{data}}$$

If main DRAM takes $50\text{ ns}$ to retrieve a cache block, the shared bus sits completely idle for dozens of clock cycles while the memory chips precharge and strobe row addresses. No other device can use the bus during this dead time.

A **split-transaction bus** (also called a **pipelined bus** or **disconnected bus protocol**) divides a single logical read into two distinct, independent bus transactions:

| Bus Timeline | Traditional Locked Bus | Split-Transaction Bus |
|---|---|---|
| **Step 1** | Master A drives Address and Read Command | Master A drives Request + Address + **Transaction Tag A**; **releases bus** |
| **Step 2** | Bus sits idle; DRAM accesses row internally (locked) | Master B drives Request + Address + **Transaction Tag B**; **releases bus** |
| **Step 3** | Bus sits idle; DRAM accesses row internally (locked) | Master C transfers a fast I/O word to Master D |
| **Step 4** | DRAM finishes; drives Data to Master A; bus freed | Memory arbitrates bus; drives Reply Data + **Tag A**; Master A latches |
| **Step 5** | Next master can finally begin arbitration | Memory arbitrates bus; drives Reply Data + **Tag B**; Master B latches |

1. **Request Phase**: The master arbitrates for the bus, transmits the memory read command, the memory address, and a unique **Transaction Tag** (identifying the requesting master and buffer), and then **immediately releases the bus**.
2. **Idle Latency**: While DRAM accesses the rows internally, the bus is completely free for other processors, DMA controllers, and graphics units to transfer data.
3. **Reply Phase**: When DRAM finishes fetching the data, the memory controller arbitrates for the bus as a master. Once granted, it places the data word onto the data bus along with the matching Transaction Tag. The original requesting master recognizes its tag, captures the data, and completes the read.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Trap: Split-transaction buses do not reduce single-read latency">
A split-transaction bus does not make an individual memory read complete faster. An individual read takes slightly longer because the system must arbitrate twice and decode transaction tags. The gain is aggregate throughput: while memory works on a read request internally, other devices use the bus for their own transfers.
</Callout>

## Comparison of bus arbitration protocols

| Arbitration Scheme | Number of Control Lines | Decision Latency | Fairness | Fault Tolerance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Daisy Chain** | 3 lines total | $O(N)$ propagation delay | Poor (distant devices starve) | Low (single broken pin breaks chain) |
| **Centralized Parallel (Fixed)** | $2N$ lines | 1 clock cycle | Poor (low priority starves) | High (isolated device failures) |
| **Centralized Parallel (Round-Robin)** | $2N$ lines | 1 clock cycle | Perfect (bounded wait time) | High (isolated device failures) |
| **Distributed (Self-Selection)** | $\log_2 N$ arbitration lines | Bitwise settling time | Flexible (ID dependent) | Maximum (no central point of failure) |

## Exam-style practice

### Problem 1: Synchronous vs. asynchronous bus bandwidth analysis

<ModelAnswer>
**Problem:** A computer architecture supports both a synchronous bus and an asynchronous bus. The bus data path is $32\text{ bits}$ wide ($4\text{ bytes}$).

1. **Part A (Synchronous Single Word):** The synchronous bus operates at $66\text{ MHz}$ (clock period $T_{\text{cyc}} = 15.15\text{ ns}$). A single 4-byte read requires $1\text{ clock cycle}$ to transmit the address, $2\text{ clock cycles}$ of memory access latency (wait states), and $1\text{ clock cycle}$ for the slave to transfer the 4-byte data word. Calculate the total time required for a 4-byte read, the effective bandwidth in megabytes per second (decimal MB/s), and the bus transfer efficiency (data cycles divided by total cycles).
2. **Part B (Synchronous Burst Mode):** The memory controller is upgraded to support a 4-word burst read ($16\text{ bytes}$). The burst requires $1\text{ address cycle}$, $2\text{ wait cycles}$ for the initial memory access, followed by $4\text{ consecutive data cycles}$ ($1\text{ cycle per 4-byte word}$). Calculate the new effective bandwidth and the percentage speedup compared to single-word reads.
3. **Part C (Asynchronous Read):** On the asynchronous bus, the master takes $15\text{ ns}$ to set up the address lines. Memory access latency is $30\text{ ns}$. The four-phase handshake requires $4\text{ propagation and logic steps}$, each taking $8\text{ ns}$ (total handshake time $32\text{ ns}$). Latched data capture takes $10\text{ ns}$. Calculate the total time to complete a single 4-byte read and its effective bandwidth in decimal MB/s.

---

**Solution:**

**Part A: Synchronous Single-Word Read**

Total clock cycles required:

$$C_{\text{total}} = C_{\text{address}} + C_{\text{wait}} + C_{\text{data}} = 1 + 2 + 1 = 4\text{ clock cycles}$$

Total read duration at $66\text{ MHz}$ ($T_{\text{cyc}} = 15.15\text{ ns}$):

$$T_{\text{read}} = 4 \times 15.15\text{ ns} = 60.60\text{ ns}$$

Effective bus bandwidth:

$$\text{Bandwidth} = \frac{4\text{ bytes}}{60.60 \times 10^{-9}\text{ s}} \approx 66.0\text{ MB/s (decimal)}$$

Bus transfer efficiency:

$$\text{Efficiency} = \frac{C_{\text{data}}}{C_{\text{total}}} = \frac{1}{4} = 25\text{\%}$$

The bus is actively moving payload data during only $25\%$ of its operating time; $75\%$ of the time is consumed by addressing and waiting.

**Part B: Synchronous Burst Mode Read**

In burst mode, 4 words ($16\text{ bytes}$) are transferred. Total clock cycles:

$$C_{\text{burst}} = C_{\text{address}} + C_{\text{wait}} + (4 \times C_{\text{data}}) = 1 + 2 + 4 = 7\text{ clock cycles}$$

Total burst duration:

$$T_{\text{burst}} = 7 \times 15.15\text{ ns} = 106.05\text{ ns}$$

Effective bandwidth:

$$\text{Bandwidth}_{\text{burst}} = \frac{16\text{ bytes}}{106.05 \times 10^{-9}\text{ s}} \approx 150.87\text{ MB/s (decimal)}$$

Speedup:

$$\text{Speedup} = \frac{\text{Bandwidth}_{\text{burst}}}{\text{Bandwidth}_{\text{single}}} = \frac{150.87}{66.0} \approx 2.29\times\text{ (a 128.6\% improvement)}$$

By amortizing the address and wait cycles over four consecutive data transfers, the bus efficiency rises to $4/7 \approx 57.1\%$.

**Part C: Asynchronous Read**

Total time for the asynchronous transfer is the direct sum of its non-overlapping physical delays:

$$T_{\text{async}} = T_{\text{address\_setup}} + T_{\text{access}} + T_{\text{handshake}} + T_{\text{latch}}$$
$$T_{\text{async}} = 15\text{ ns} + 30\text{ ns} + 32\text{ ns} + 10\text{ ns} = 87\text{ ns}$$

Effective asynchronous bandwidth:

$$\text{Bandwidth}_{\text{async}} = \frac{4\text{ bytes}}{87 \times 10^{-9}\text{ s}} \approx 45.98\text{ MB/s (decimal)}$$

Comparing the two: The synchronous bus ($60.60\text{ ns}$, $66.0\text{ MB/s}$) is faster than the asynchronous bus ($87\text{ ns}$, $45.98\text{ MB/s}$) for single-word transfers, because the asynchronous bus must pay $32\text{ ns}$ of four-phase handshaking delays.
</ModelAnswer>

### Problem 2: Bus arbitration analysis: daisy chain vs. round-robin fairness

<ModelAnswer>
**Problem:** A computer system has 4 bus masters: Master 0 (GPU), Master 1 (NVMe SSD), Master 2 (Gigabit Network), and Master 3 (CPU). Each bus transaction takes exactly $100\text{ ns}$ of bus transmission time.

1. **Part A (Daisy Chain Starvation):** The four devices are connected in a physical daisy chain in the order Master 0 $\to$ Master 1 $\to$ Master 2 $\to$ Master 3. Master 0 requires bus access $70\%$ of the total available time, and Master 1 requires bus access $25\%$ of the total time. If bus requests arrive independently, what percentage of the total bus bandwidth is left available for Master 2 and Master 3? What happens to the CPU (Master 3) if Master 0 increases its bus demand to $80\%$ and Master 1 to $25\%$?
2. **Part B (Round-Robin Bounded Waiting Time):** The system replaces daisy chaining with a centralized round-robin arbiter. Each master is granted at most one $100\text{ ns}$ transaction per turn before the arbiter advances to the next requesting master. If Master 3 asserts a bus request, what is the guaranteed worst-case waiting time until Master 3's transaction begins, assuming all other three masters are also demanding the bus?
3. **Part C (Bandwidth Allocation):** Under continuous maximum load where all four masters request the bus constantly, what fraction and percentage of total bus bandwidth does each master receive under round-robin arbitration?

---

**Solution:**

**Part A: Daisy Chain Allocation and Starvation**

In a daisy chain, upstream masters hold absolute priority over downstream masters.

- Master 0 receives first choice and consumes $70\%$ of bus bandwidth.
- The remaining bandwidth available to downstream devices is $100\% - 70\% = 30\%$.
- Master 1 consumes $25\%$ out of this remaining capacity.
- The remaining bandwidth available for Master 2 and Master 3 combined is:

$$\text{Bandwidth}_{\text{remaining}} = 30\text{\%} - 25\text{\%} = 5\text{\%}$$

Master 2 and Master 3 must share the remaining $5\%$.

If Master 0 increases its demand to $80\%$ and Master 1 requires $25\%$:

$$\text{Demand}_{\text{M0}} + \text{Demand}_{\text{M1}} = 80\text{\%} + 25\text{\%} = 105\text{\%} > 100\text{\%}$$

Master 0 takes $80\%$, Master 1 takes the remaining $20\%$ (leaving Master 1 partially starved), and zero bandwidth ($0\%$) reaches Master 2 and Master 3. The CPU (Master 3) is completely starved of memory access, freezing all foreground user computation.

**Part B: Round-Robin Bounded Waiting Time**

In a 4-master round-robin arbiter, after serving Master 3, the arbiter services Master 0, Master 1, and Master 2 in rotation.

The worst-case scenario occurs when Master 3 asserts its request right after the arbiter grant has already passed Master 3 and started serving Master 0.

Before Master 3 can receive another grant, each of the other three masters can execute at most one $100\text{ ns}$ transaction:

$$T_{\text{wait\_max}} = (N - 1) \times T_{\text{transaction}} = (4 - 1) \times 100\text{ ns} = 3 \times 100\text{ ns} = 300\text{ ns}$$

Master 3 is guaranteed to begin its transaction within at most $300\text{ ns}$. Starvation cannot occur.

**Part C: Round-Robin Bandwidth Allocation**

When all four masters continuously request the bus, the round-robin arbiter cycles through them in strict sequence:

$$\text{Sequence}: \text{M0} \to \text{M1} \to \text{M2} \to \text{M3} \to \text{M0} \to \dots$$

Each master receives exactly one transaction per 4-transaction cycle.

$$\text{Bandwidth Share} = \frac{1}{4} = 25\text{\%}$$

Each master receives exactly $25\%$ of total bus bandwidth, providing complete fairness regardless of device priority or physical slot position.
</ModelAnswer>
