# Discrete Logarithms

The last lesson showed that $a^k \bmod p$ is fast to compute. This lesson is about going backwards, finding $k$ when you know the result. Nobody knows a fast way to do that in general, and public-key cryptography is built on that gap.

## Logarithms, modulo a prime

An ordinary logarithm undoes a power: if $y = x^{\log_x y}$, then $\log_x y$ is the exponent. Three rules go with it:

$$
\log_x 1 = 0, \qquad \log_x (yz) = \log_x y + \log_x z, \qquad \log_x (y^r) = r \log_x y
$$

Now take a primitive root $a$ of a prime $p$. Its powers hit every value from 1 to $p - 1$ exactly once, so for any $b$ in that range there's a unique exponent $k$ with

$$
b \equiv a^k \pmod p, \qquad 0 \le k \le p - 2
$$

This $k$ is the **discrete logarithm** of $b$ to the base $a$. The same rules hold, with the exponents worked out modulo $\phi(p)$.

## A small example

It also works for some non-prime moduli. For $n = 9$, $\phi(9) = 6$ and 2 is a primitive root:

$$
2^0 = 1,\ 2^1 = 2,\ 2^2 = 4,\ 2^3 = 8,\ 2^4 \equiv 7,\ 2^5 \equiv 5 \pmod 9
$$

Rearranged, that gives a table of logs:

| Number | 1 | 2 | 4 | 5 | 7 | 8 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Log | 0 | 1 | 2 | 5 | 4 | 3 |

## A one-way street

| Direction | Problem | Effort |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Forward | Compute $a^k \bmod p$ | Fast, about $\log k$ multiplications |
| Backward | Find $k$ from $a^k \bmod p$ | No known fast method on classical computers |

## Integer factorization

There's a second problem of this kind. Given a composite number $N$, find integers $p$ and $q$ with $p \cdot q = N$. Multiplying is easy, but factoring a product of two large primes is not. RSA depends on this one, in the same way that Diffie-Hellman depends on discrete logs.
