# What Is Cryptography?

Everything from the last module was preparation for this one. Number theory gives us operations that are easy to do and hard to undo without a secret, and cryptography puts them to work.

## The vocabulary

- **Cryptography** (from the Greek for "hidden writing") is the study of how to conceal information in a ciphertext, so that only the legitimate users holding the key can recover it.
- **Cryptanalysis** is the science of recovering information from ciphertext without knowing the key.
- **Cryptology** is the broader field that covers both.

Modern cryptography solves two main security problems:

1. **Privacy** stops unauthorised parties from reading messages sent over a public channel. The sender can be sure that only the intended receiver can read it.
2. **Authentication** stops unauthorised parties from injecting messages. The receiver can be sure who sent it.

Cryptanalysis also helped create the modern computer. At Bletchley Park in the Second World War, Alan Turing helped design the Bombe, an electromechanical machine for breaking Enigma, and Tommy Flowers built Colossus, one of the first programmable electronic computers, to attack another German cipher.

## Two kinds of system

Cryptosystems split into two families:

- **Secret-key** (or symmetric) systems use the same key to encrypt and decrypt.
- **Public-key** (or asymmetric) systems use a public key to encrypt and a different private key to decrypt.

## The notation

A cryptosystem has these parts:

- $M$, the **message space**: the plaintext strings to be encrypted.
- $C$, the **ciphertext space**: the encrypted strings.
- $K$, the **key space**: the possible keys, including an encryption key $e_k$ and a decryption key $d_k$.
- $E$, the encryption algorithm: $E_{e_k}(m) = c$.
- $D$, the decryption algorithm: $D_{d_k}(c) = m$.

The two algorithms have to undo each other, for every message:

$$
D_{d_k}(E_{e_k}(m)) = m
$$

## Secret-key systems
