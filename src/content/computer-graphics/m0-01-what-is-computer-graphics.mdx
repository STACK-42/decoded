# What Computer Graphics Is

<Epigraph quote="A display connected to a digital computer gives us a chance to gain familiarity with concepts not realizable in the physical world. It is a looking glass into a mathematical wonderland." by="Ivan E. Sutherland" source="The Ultimate Display, Proceedings of IFIP Congress, 1965" />

Everything you see on a screen right now, these letters included, was drawn by a program that decided the color of every dot. **Computer graphics** is the study of how computers create, store, and manipulate images, and how they display those images on electronic screens.

This course begins with the smallest unit, a single dot, and builds up to the algorithms that decide which dots to light when drawing a straight line.

## A screen is a grid of dots

Looking closely at a digital display, or examining it under magnification, reveals that the smooth picture is actually a grid of tiny squares. Each square is a **pixel**, short for *picture element*: the smallest controllable unit of a display. A pixel displays exactly one color at a time.

All digital imagery builds on this foundation. Drag the slider below to see how a smooth curve resolves into discrete square steps:

<PixelZoom />

This demonstrates the core challenge of computer graphics. The geometric shapes we want to draw (circles, lines, and text glyphs) are continuous mathematical curves. The display, however, can only light discrete square cells. Computer graphics is largely the work of choosing which squares best represent those continuous shapes.

## Four words that sound alike

People often use these terms as synonyms, but each has a distinct technical meaning:

- An **image** is any visual content stored digitally: a photo, a scan, a screenshot, or a digital painting. Inside a computer, an image is stored as a grid of pixel values. File formats such as JPEG, PNG, GIF and BMP are all ways to store images.
- A **picture** is a standalone visual representation created by drawing, painting, photography or printing. The word typically refers to a single complete piece of art or a framed work.
- A **graph** is a visual representation of data: charts, plots and diagrams that display relationships or trends in numbers.
- A **photo** (short for photograph) is an image captured by a camera with an optical lens and sensor, recording a real-world scene.

Every digital photo on your phone is an image, but many images are not photos. A financial chart generated in a spreadsheet is both an image and a graph, with no camera involved.

## Interactive and passive graphics

Computer graphics falls into two broad categories based on user control.

**Interactive graphics** provides a two-way exchange between user and computer. You provide input with a mouse, touch screen, keyboard or game controller, and the program redraws the picture in response, often dozens of times each second. Video games, interactive maps, buttons that highlight on hover, and touch drawing applications are all interactive.

**Passive graphics** (or non-interactive graphics) displays pictures without responding to ongoing user input while running. A chart in a PDF document, a printed logo, a diagram on a static web page, and a simple screen saver are passive. Passive graphics can still include motion: a recorded film or looping animation moves across time, but the viewer cannot alter its path.

<InteractiveVsPassive />

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common mix-up">
Motion alone does not make graphics interactive. A film moves continuously on screen, but it is passive because the viewer cannot alter what happens. The distinguishing factor is input response: does the picture change based on your actions?

Some course notes classify any animated sequence as interactive, citing movie animation or animated weather forecasts as examples. If an exam follows that phrasing, use its expected answer, but understand the real distinction used in practice.
</Callout>

## Where it's used

<ApplicationsGrid>
<PracticalApplication title="Design and engineering" tool="CAD">
Computer-aided design tools let engineers and architects construct precise blueprints and 3D models of components, buildings and consumer products before manufacturing begins.
</PracticalApplication>
<PracticalApplication title="User interfaces" tool="Every GUI">
Windows, icons, buttons and menus are graphical elements. A graphical user interface (GUI) replaces typed terminal commands with visual controls you can point to and tap.
</PracticalApplication>
<PracticalApplication title="Games, film and virtual reality" tool="Game engines, renderers">
Video games redraw the entire screen 60 or more times per second in response to player actions. Animated films **render** each frame by computing light and shadows from a 3D model, a process that can take hours per frame on a server cluster.
</PracticalApplication>
<PracticalApplication title="Data visualization" tool="Charts, maps, medical scans">
Converting numerical tables into charts, weather maps or MRI scans lets people spot trends and anomalies visually rather than reading columns of numbers.
</PracticalApplication>
<PracticalApplication title="Digital art" tool="Digital painting, generative tools">
Artwork created using digital tools, including digital illustrations, 3D character models and programmatically generated art.
</PracticalApplication>
<PracticalApplication title="Image processing" tool="Photo editors, camera pipelines">
Manipulating existing images to enhance quality or extract data: sharpening soft photos, removing sensor noise, or identifying faces.
</PracticalApplication>
<PracticalApplication title="Training and simulators" tool="Flight simulators">
Flight simulators and interactive training environments let trainees practice complex procedures using a virtual simulation of real equipment.
</PracticalApplication>
</ApplicationsGrid>
