# The CRT and the Raster Scan

Flat screens replaced them long ago, but the **cathode-ray tube (CRT)** remains where computer graphics courses begin. The CRT drew images row by row from top to bottom, establishing the scan pattern that still feeds modern digital displays. The core vocabulary of modern computer screens comes directly from how a CRT operates.

Whatever its underlying technology, the screen component of a computer is called the **display**, the **monitor**, or the **video display unit (VDU)**. In this module we look at how the CRT scans an image onto glass in two ways (raster scan here and random scan next), followed by modern flat panels (LCD, LED-backlit LCD, OLED, and plasma).

## Inside the tube

A CRT is a sealed glass vacuum tube. Three main parts create, aim, and light up the picture:

1. **An electron gun** at the narrow rear of the tube holds a heated, negatively charged metal filament called a **cathode**. The cathode emits electrons, and the gun accelerates and focuses them into a narrow stream called a **cathode ray**. A color CRT uses three separate guns, one aimed for red, one for green, and one for blue.
2. **Deflection coils** mounted around the neck of the tube generate magnetic fields that bend the beam horizontally and vertically to reach any coordinate on the screen. Some laboratory tubes steer the beam with electric fields between metal plates, known as **electrostatic deflection**, but television sets and computer monitors use magnetic coils.
3. **The screen** at the wide front of the tube is coated on the inside with **phosphor**, a crystalline material that glows when electrons strike it. A color screen arranges red, green, and blue phosphor dots into microscopic triads, where each triad forms one pixel. A thin metal mask just behind the glass ensures that electrons from each gun can strike only their matching color dot.

Where the beam strikes the phosphor coating, the spot lights up. Sending a stronger flow of electrons (a higher **beam current**) produces a brighter glow at that point.

## Persistence and refresh

Phosphor does not glow indefinitely. It dims within a fraction of a second after the electron beam moves away. The duration that a phosphor continues to emit light is called its **persistence**.

Because phosphor dims quickly, the screen cannot be drawn once and left static. The beam must trace over the entire screen repeatedly before the glow fades. This continuous redrawing is called **refreshing** the screen, and the number of full redraws per second is the **refresh rate**, measured in hertz (Hz) or frames per second. CRTs typically refresh between 60 Hz and 85 Hz. At 60 Hz, the display has $1 \div 60 \approx 0.0167$ seconds, or roughly 16.7 milliseconds, to draw an entire frame from top to bottom.

Two rates describe the sweep of the beam:

- The **vertical refresh rate** is the number of complete frames drawn per second from top to bottom, such as 60 Hz.
- The **horizontal refresh rate** (or scan rate) is the number of individual rows drawn per second from left to right. Because each frame contains hundreds of rows, this rate is much higher. A display with 480 visible rows refreshed at 60 Hz already requires $480 \times 60 = 28{,}800$ rows per second. Counting the extra blanking rows swept while the beam moves back between rows and frames (roughly 525 line periods per frame, $525 \times 60 = 31{,}500$), a standard 640 × 480 monitor runs at a horizontal scan rate of roughly 31.5 kHz ($1\text{ kHz} = 1{,}000\text{ Hz}$).

The graphics adapter and the monitor set these rates together. Large screens make flicker easy to spot in peripheral vision, and higher resolutions pack more scan lines into every frame. Both push systems toward higher refresh rates to prevent visible **flicker**, which occurs when phosphor dims noticeably between sweeps and causes eye strain. A standard guideline recommended at least 60 Hz at 640 × 480 and at least 85 Hz at 1600 × 1200.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Persistence is a balance">
Long persistence reduces flicker but smears motion because earlier frames continue to glow when new ones arrive. Short persistence keeps moving objects sharp but requires a higher refresh rate to avoid visible flicker.
</Callout>

## The raster scan

A **raster-scan display** sweeps the electron beam across the screen in a fixed, repeating pattern:

1. Start at the top-left corner.
2. Sweep from left to right along one row, called a **scan line**, varying the beam's strength for each pixel's brightness and turning it off where pixels stay dark.
3. At the end of the row, turn the beam off and move it back to the left edge of the next row down. This return stroke is the **horizontal retrace**.
4. Repeat this sequence until the bottom row is complete.
5. Turn the beam off and return it to the top-left corner to begin the next frame. This return trip is the **vertical retrace**.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Retrace draws nothing">
The beam is blanked during both retraces, so it draws nothing while traveling back. The two retraces differ in frequency: a horizontal retrace occurs at the end of every row, hundreds of times per frame, while a vertical retrace occurs once per frame.
</Callout>

## The frame buffer

As the beam sweeps across the screen, the display system needs a record of which pixels to illuminate and at what intensity. That record is stored in the **frame buffer** (also called the **refresh buffer**): a dedicated block of memory that holds one color value for every pixel on the display.

The display's output hardware (the **display controller**) reads the frame buffer row by row in lockstep with the electron beam, while graphics software updates the image by writing values into this buffer. In the interactive visualizer above, the grid of 0s and 1s beside the screen represents the frame buffer for the house, storing one bit per pixel.

The memory required for a frame buffer follows directly from the [image size calculation](/courses/computer-graphics/image-size): horizontal resolution × vertical resolution × bytes per pixel. For example, a 1920 × 1080 display at 32 bits (4 bytes) per pixel requires $1920 \times 1080 \times 4 = 8{,}294{,}400$ bytes, which is $8{,}294{,}400 \div 1024 \div 1024 \approx 7.9$ MB for a single frame. This buffer can reside in dedicated video memory on a graphics card or in reserved system [RAM](/courses/computer-organization/ram-and-rom).

## Scan conversion and the display processor

Software rarely specifies pictures pixel by pixel. Instead, programs issue geometric commands such as drawing a line between two coordinates or filling a polygon. Converting these geometric shapes into pixel values in the frame buffer is called **scan conversion** (or **rasterization**). The [line-drawing algorithms](/courses/computer-graphics/dda) later in this course perform this exact task.

To free the main [CPU](/courses/computer-organization/inside-the-cpu) from calculating every pixel, computer systems use a dedicated **display processor** (also called a **display processing unit**). Its primary job is scan conversion: filling shapes, drawing styled lines, and writing pixel values into the frame buffer so the display controller can read them out. Modern **GPUs** (graphics processing units) developed directly from these early display processors.

<PracticalApplication title="Modern screens" tool="LCD, OLED, HDMI">
Flat panels (like LCD and OLED, covered in the flat-panels lesson) do not use electron beams, yet digital video cables like HDMI still transmit pixels from the frame buffer row by row, from top to bottom, at 60 Hz or higher. The short pause between consecutive frames is still called the vertical blanking interval, directly preserving the timing of CRT retrace. In games, screen tearing occurs when the GPU overwrites the frame buffer while the display controller is halfway through sending a frame to the screen.
</PracticalApplication>
