# Resolution: PPI and DPI

In everyday speech, people use the word "resolution" for two different things, and separating them prevents the most common mistake in this topic:

- **Image dimensions** (often loosely called "resolution"): how many pixels wide and tall an image is, like 1200 × 800. The file fixes this count.
- **Image resolution** in the strict sense: how densely those pixels are packed on paper or screen, measured in **pixels per inch** (**PPI**). This sets how large an image prints at a given quality.

The same 1200 × 800 photo can print as a crisp postcard or a grainy poster. The pixels do not change; only how tightly they are packed does. (**Screen resolution**, how many pixels a physical display contains, gets [its own lesson](/courses/computer-graphics/screen-resolution).)

## PPI: pixels per inch

**PPI (pixels per inch)** counts how many pixels fit along one linear inch. More pixels per inch means each pixel takes up less physical space, so edges and curves look smoother at that physical size.

To find the printed size of an image, divide its pixels by the PPI:

$$
\text{inches} = \frac{\text{pixels}}{\text{PPI}}
$$

Consider a 1200 × 800 image at 72 PPI (a classic baseline density for older monitors):

$$
\frac{1200}{72} \approx 16.667 \text{ in wide}, \qquad \frac{800}{72} \approx 11.111 \text{ in tall}
$$

Print the same image at 300 PPI (the standard benchmark for sharp handheld prints):

$$
\frac{1200}{300} = 4 \text{ in wide}, \qquad \frac{800}{300} \approx 2.667 \text{ in tall}
$$

Raising the PPI from 72 to 300 shrinks the print to about a quarter of the width ($300 \div 72 \approx 4.17$), packing the same pixels closer together. To convert inches to centimeters, multiply by 2.54: 4 inches is 10.16 cm. Try it:

## Working backwards: how many pixels do I need?

Flip the formula when you know the print size you want:

$$
\text{pixels} = \text{inches} \times \text{PPI}
$$

To print a 4 × 6 inch photo at a quality of 240 PPI, you need:

$$
240 \times 4 = 960 \text{ pixels wide}, \qquad 240 \times 6 = 1440 \text{ pixels tall}
$$

An image with fewer pixels will either print smaller or have to be stretched. Stretching causes blur because the software must invent the extra pixels by averaging neighboring ones (**resampling**), and averages are softer than real detail.

## The third question: what PPI will I get?

When you know both the pixel count and the physical print size, divide pixels by inches to find the density:

$$
\text{PPI} = \frac{\text{pixels}}{\text{inches}}
$$

A 1200-pixel-wide photo printed 8 inches wide comes out at $1200 \div 8 = 150$ PPI. That looks fine for a poster seen from across a room, but soft up close. The same formula answers three questions, depending on which two numbers you already have.

<Callout tone="orange" label="More PPI is not more detail">
Typing a larger PPI into an image's file settings does not add pixels or create detail. It only shrinks the print size so those existing pixels pack closer together. If you force the print back up to the original size, the effective PPI drops right back down.
</Callout>

## DPI: dots per inch

**DPI (dots per inch)** belongs to physical printers. While screens add red, green, and blue light, printers spray tiny drops of cyan, magenta, yellow, and black ink onto white paper. It takes a cluster of overlapping ink dots to imitate one pixel's color, so printers need a higher dot density than the image's pixel density. DPI counts how many ink dots the printer places along one linear inch: inkjet printers typically lay down 300 to 720 DPI, and laser printers reach 600 to 2,400 DPI.

| | PPI | DPI |
|---|---|---|
| Measures | pixels in an image or on a screen | ink dots a printer lays down |
| Belongs to | the digital file or display | the physical printer |
| Set when | preparing an image file | selecting print quality |

<Callout tone="orange" label="Used interchangeably anyway">
Many software tools and textbooks write "DPI" when they mean PPI, especially for screens and print-ready files. In precise technical usage, write PPI for digital images and screens, and DPI for physical printers.
</Callout>

<PracticalApplication title="Design and printing" tool="Photoshop's Image Size, print shops">
A print shop often asks for "300 DPI" files. What they mean is enough pixels for 300 pixels per inch at the final printed size. Changing the PPI field in an image editor without **resampling** (adding or removing pixels) changes only the printed dimensions, exactly as in the calculator above.
