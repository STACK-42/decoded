# Resolution: PPI and DPI

The word "resolution" gets used for two different things, and mixing them up is the most common mistake in this topic:

- **Pixel dimensions**: how many pixels an image has, like 1200 × 800. This count is fixed by the file.
- **Resolution** in the strict sense: how densely those pixels are packed, measured as **pixels per inch** (or **pixel density**). This decides how big the image looks once it is shown or printed.

The same 1200 × 800 photo can be printed as a postcard or a poster. The pixels do not change; only how tightly they are packed does.

Course notes often distinguish between **image resolution** (the pixel density in PPI) and **screen resolution** (how many pixels a display has). Do not confuse image resolution with **image dimensions**, which is the count of columns and rows like 640 × 480. Screen resolution gets [its own lesson](/courses/computer-graphics/screen-resolution).

## PPI: pixels per inch

**PPI (pixels per inch)** counts how many pixels fit along one inch. It describes digital images and screens. More pixels per inch means each pixel is smaller, so the picture looks sharper and lines look smoother.

To find the printed size of an image, divide its pixels by the PPI:

$$
\text{inches} = \frac{\text{pixels}}{\text{PPI}}
$$

A 1200 × 800 image at 72 PPI:

$$
\frac{1200}{72} \approx 16.667 \text{ in wide}, \qquad \frac{800}{72} \approx 11.111 \text{ in tall}
$$

The same image at 300 PPI:

$$
\frac{1200}{300} = 4 \text{ in wide}, \qquad \frac{800}{300} \approx 2.667 \text{ in tall}
$$

Raising the PPI from 72 to 300 shrinks the print to about a quarter of the width, since $300 \div 72 \approx 4.17$. It is the same file, with its pixels packed closer together. If an exam question asks for centimetres, multiply inches by 2.54: 4 inches is 10.16 cm. Try it:

## Working backwards: how many pixels do I need?

Flip the formula when you know the print size you want:

$$
\text{pixels} = \text{inches} \times \text{PPI}
$$

To print a 4 × 6 inch photo at a quality of 240 PPI, you need

$$
240 \times 4 = 960 \text{ pixels wide}, \qquad 240 \times 6 = 1440 \text{ pixels tall}
$$

A smaller image will either print smaller or get stretched. Stretching causes blur because the software has to invent missing pixels by averaging neighbors, and averages are softer than real detail.

## The third question: what PPI will I get?

When you know both the pixel count and the physical print size, divide pixels by inches to find the density:

$$
\text{PPI} = \frac{\text{pixels}}{\text{inches}}
$$

A 1200-pixel-wide photo printed 8 inches wide comes out at $1200 \div 8 = 150$ PPI. That looks fine for a poster seen from across a room, but soft up close. The same formula answers three questions, depending on which two numbers you already have.

<Callout tone="orange" label="More PPI is not more detail">
Typing a larger PPI into an image's settings does not make it sharper. Without new pixels, it only makes the print smaller. Real detail comes from the pixel count. PPI only decides how tightly those pixels are packed.
</Callout>

## DPI: dots per inch

**DPI (dots per inch)** belongs to physical printers. A printer does not print pixels directly. It sprays tiny dots of ink, and it takes multiple dots of cyan, magenta, yellow and black to reproduce one pixel's color. DPI counts how many ink dots the printer can place along one inch. Inkjet printers manage roughly 300 to 720 DPI, and laser printers reach 600 to 2,400 DPI.

| | PPI | DPI |
|---|---|---|
| Measures | pixels in an image or on a screen | ink dots a printer lays down |
| Belongs to | the digital file or display | the physical printer |
| Set when | preparing an image file | selecting print quality |

<Callout tone="orange" label="Used interchangeably anyway">
Many software tools and lecture notes write "DPI" when they mean PPI, especially for screens. On an exam, write PPI for digital images and screens, and DPI for printers.
</Callout>
