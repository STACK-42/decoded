# The DDA Line Algorithm

The [naive algorithm](/courses/computer-graphics/slope-and-the-line-equation#the-naive-algorithm-just-use-the-equation) suffered from two primary drawbacks: it left visible gaps in steep lines, and it required a floating-point multiplication for every single pixel. The **digital differential analyzer (DDA)** resolves both issues.

## Idea 1: incremental addition instead of multiplication

Examining the slope-intercept equation as $x$ advances by single unit steps reveals an efficient recursive relationship:

$$
y_{k+1} = m(x_k + 1) + c = (m x_k + c) + m = y_k + m
$$

Each subsequent $y$ value is obtained by adding slope $m$ to the previous $y$ value. This eliminates multiplication entirely during the inner rendering loop: the algorithm initializes at the starting endpoint and updates coordinates using repeated addition. Formulating successive values from previous terms is called an **incremental** method, and DDA is its classic graphic formulation.

## Idea 2: step along the dominant axis

To eliminate gaps, the algorithm steps by exactly one pixel along whichever coordinate axis spans the greater distance. The coordinate along the secondary axis changes by a fractional increment on each step:

$$
\text{steps} = \max(\lvert \Delta x \rvert, \lvert \Delta y \rvert)
\qquad
x_{\text{inc}} = \frac{\Delta x}{\text{steps}}
\qquad
y_{\text{inc}} = \frac{\Delta y}{\text{steps}}
$$

The dominant axis advances by $\pm 1$ pixel per step, while the secondary axis changes by a fraction with absolute value $\le 1$. Every row or column spanned by the line receives exactly one pixel, ensuring a continuous path without gaps.

## The three cases

Although the unified formula handles any line orientation, graphics textbooks commonly analyze DDA across three cases based on slope magnitude:

| Case | Condition | Steps | x increment | y increment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $\lvert m \rvert < 1$, gentle | $\lvert \Delta x \rvert$ | $\pm 1$ | $m$ |
| 2 | $\lvert m \rvert = 1$, diagonal | $\lvert \Delta x \rvert = \lvert \Delta y \rvert$ | $\pm 1$ | $\pm 1$ |
| 3 | $\lvert m \rvert > 1$, steep | $\lvert \Delta y \rvert$ | $1/m$ | $\pm 1$ |

When a line extends from right to left or top to bottom, the step signs naturally become negative, matching the signs of $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$.

## The algorithm

1. Compute coordinate differences $\Delta x = x_2 - x_1$ and $\Delta y = y_2 - y_1$.
2. Determine total steps: $\text{steps} = \max(\lvert\Delta x\rvert, \lvert\Delta y\rvert)$.
3. Compute coordinate increments: $x_{\text{inc}} = \Delta x / \text{steps}$ and $y_{\text{inc}} = \Delta y / \text{steps}$.
4. Initialize floating-point coordinates at the start point: $x = x_1$, $y = y_1$.
5. Loop $\text{steps} + 1$ iterations (the extra iteration plots the initial endpoint): illuminate pixel $(\text{round}(x), \text{round}(y))$, then update $x \leftarrow x + x_{\text{inc}}$ and $y \leftarrow y + y_{\text{inc}}$.

## Example 1: a gentle line, (5, 4) to (12, 7)

For endpoints $(5, 4)$ and $(12, 7)$, $\Delta x = 7$ and $\Delta y = 3$, giving $m = 3/7 \approx 0.429$. Because $\lvert m \rvert < 1$, this falls under Case 1: the algorithm executes 7 steps, incrementing $x$ by 1 and $y$ by $3/7 \approx 0.429$ per iteration:

| k | x | y (exact) | pixel |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 5 | 4 | (5, 4) |
| 1 | 6 | 4.429 | (6, 4) |
| 2 | 7 | 4.857 | (7, 5) |
| 3 | 8 | 5.286 | (8, 5) |
| 4 | 9 | 5.714 | (9, 6) |
| 5 | 10 | 6.143 | (10, 6) |
| 6 | 11 | 6.571 | (11, 7) |
| 7 | 12 | 7 | (12, 7) |

<Callout tone="orange" label="Cumulative error from rounding slope increments">
Rounding the slope to a coarse decimal like $0.4$ before executing the loop introduces cumulative error. With increment $0.4$, $y$ advances as $4.0, 4.4, 4.8, 5.2, 5.6, 6.0, 6.4, 6.8$, failing to reach the final endpoint value of $7.0$. At $x = 11$, the truncated sum $6.4$ rounds to pixel $(11, 6)$ rather than the correct $(11, 7)$. Each addition accumulates truncation errors; always maintain slope increments as exact rational fractions or high-precision floating-point values.
</Callout>

## Example 2: a steep line, (5, 7) to (10, 15)

For endpoints $(5, 7)$ and $(10, 15)$, $\Delta x = 5$ and $\Delta y = 8$, giving $m = 8/5 = 1.6$. Because $\lvert m \rvert > 1$, this falls under Case 3: $y$ is the dominant axis spanning 8 steps, while $x$ advances by $1/m = 5/8 = 0.625$ per step:

| k | x (exact) | y | pixel |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 5 | 7 | (5, 7) |
| 1 | 5.625 | 8 | (6, 8) |
| 2 | 6.25 | 9 | (6, 9) |
| 3 | 6.875 | 10 | (7, 10) |
| 4 | 7.5 | 11 | (8, 11) |
| 5 | 8.125 | 12 | (8, 12) |
| 6 | 8.75 | 13 | (9, 13) |
| 7 | 9.375 | 14 | (9, 14) |
| 8 | 10 | 15 | (10, 15) |

At iteration $k = 4$, $x = 7.5$ lies exactly equidistant between column 7 and column 8. Following the convention of rounding half values upward yields pixel $(8, 11)$.

## Example 3: diagonals, (3, 5) to (8, 10) and back

When $\Delta x = \Delta y = 5$ (Case 2), both coordinates advance by exactly 1 on every step without requiring fractional rounding: $(3, 5), (4, 6), (5, 7), (6, 8), (7, 9), (8, 10)$.

Reversing direction from $(8, 10)$ to $(3, 5)$ yields $\Delta x = \Delta y = -5$, so both increments evaluate to $-1$. The identical sequence of six pixels is plotted in reverse order, demonstrating that the parametric step equations naturally handle arbitrary line directions.

## Example 4: horizontal, (2, 2) to (9, 2)

When $\Delta y = 0$, slope $m = 0$. The algorithm computes $\text{steps} = 7$, with $x_{\text{inc}} = 1$ and $y_{\text{inc}} = 0$, generating $(2, 2), (3, 2), \dots, (9, 2)$ (8 total pixels). Vertical lines work symmetrically by exchanging axes, handling the condition where the naive algorithm failed due to division by zero.

## Example 5: (2, 2) to (8, 5), and a homework line

Some curricula introduce this incremental procedure under the label "naive line-drawing algorithm" (finding $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$, setting $\text{steps} = \max(\lvert\Delta x\rvert, \lvert\Delta y\rvert)$, and repeatedly adding increments). In computer graphics literature, this formulation is the DDA algorithm.

Tracing from $(2, 2)$ to $(8, 5)$: $\Delta x = 6$, $\Delta y = 3$, giving 6 steps with increments $x_{\text{inc}} = 6/6 = 1.0$ and $y_{\text{inc}} = 3/6 = 0.5$.

| k | x | y (exact) | pixel |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 2 | 2 | (2, 2) |
| 1 | 3 | 2.5 | (3, 3) |
| 2 | 4 | 3 | (4, 3) |
| 3 | 5 | 3.5 | (5, 4) |
| 4 | 6 | 4 | (6, 4) |
| 5 | 7 | 4.5 | (7, 5) |
| 6 | 8 | 5 | (8, 5) |

<Callout tone="orange" label="Points vs discrete pixels">
Listing coordinates like $(3, 2.5)$ identifies mathematical locations along the continuous line, not pixel entries in a frame buffer. The final step in DDA is always rounding each calculated coordinate to its discrete pixel center. Rounding half values upward maps $(3, 2.5)$ to pixel $(3, 3)$.
</Callout>

Tracing a line from $(3, 1)$ to $(9, 6)$: $\Delta x = 6$ and $\Delta y = 5$, giving $\text{steps} = 6$, $x_{\text{inc}} = 1.0$, and $y_{\text{inc}} = 5/6 \approx 0.833$. The continuous $y$ values evaluate to $1.0, 1.833, 2.667, 3.5, 4.333, 5.167,$ and $6.0$, rounding to pixels $(3, 1), (4, 2), (5, 3), (6, 4), (7, 4), (8, 5),$ and $(9, 6)$.

## Limitations of DDA

While DDA eliminates gaps and replaces inner-loop multiplication with addition, it has notable limitations:

- It relies on [floating-point](/courses/logic-design/codes-and-floating-point#floating-point-very-large-and-very-small-numbers) arithmetic and requires an explicit rounding call per pixel, which consumes processor cycles.
- When rendering long line segments, repeated accumulation of floating-point inaccuracies causes calculated positions to gradually drift away from the true line.

<PracticalApplication title="Rasterization" tool="GPUs, game engines">
The principle of incremental coordinate evaluation remains ubiquitous in computer graphics. GPUs rasterize triangles by interpolating edge coordinates incrementally across scan lines. Grid-traversal DDA variants are also standard in ray casting, tracing rays step by step across voxel grids or 2D tile maps in game engines.
</PracticalApplication>
