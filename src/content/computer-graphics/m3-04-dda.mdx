# The DDA Line Algorithm

The [naive algorithm](/courses/computer-graphics/slope-and-the-line-equation#the-naive-algorithm-just-use-the-equation) left gaps on steep lines, divided by zero on vertical lines, and needed a floating-point multiplication for every pixel. The **digital differential analyzer (DDA)** solves all three problems. Its name comes from early computing devices ("differential analyzers") that solved equations by accumulating small increments step by step.

## Idea 1: incremental addition instead of multiplication

Examining the slope-intercept equation as $x$ advances by single unit steps reveals an efficient recursive relationship:

$$
y_{k+1} = m(x_k + 1) + c = (m x_k + c) + m = y_k + m
$$

Each subsequent $y$ value is obtained by adding slope $m$ to the previous $y$ value. This eliminates multiplication entirely during the inner rendering loop: the algorithm initializes at the starting endpoint and updates coordinates using repeated addition. Formulating successive values from previous terms is called an **incremental** method, and DDA is its classic graphic formulation.

## Idea 2: step along the dominant axis

To eliminate gaps, the algorithm steps by one pixel along whichever axis spans the greater distance, and advances the other axis by a fractional step:

$$
\text{steps} = \max(\lvert \Delta x \rvert, \lvert \Delta y \rvert)
\qquad
x_{\text{inc}} = \frac{\Delta x}{\text{steps}}
\qquad
y_{\text{inc}} = \frac{\Delta y}{\text{steps}}
$$

When $\lvert \Delta x \rvert \ge \lvert \Delta y \rvert$, dividing by $\text{steps} = \lvert \Delta x \rvert$ makes $x_{\text{inc}} = \pm 1$ and $y_{\text{inc}} = \Delta y / \lvert \Delta x \rvert$ (which has size $\le 1$). When $\lvert \Delta y \rvert > \lvert \Delta x \rvert$, dividing by $\lvert \Delta y \rvert$ makes $y_{\text{inc}} = \pm 1$ and $x_{\text{inc}} = \Delta x / \lvert \Delta y \rvert$ (again size $\le 1$). Because the dominant axis always moves by 1 pixel per step, every row or column along the line gets a pixel with no gaps.

## The three cases

Although the unified formula handles any line orientation, graphics textbooks commonly analyze DDA across three cases based on slope magnitude:

| Case | Condition | Steps | x increment | y increment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $\lvert m \rvert < 1$, gentle | $\lvert \Delta x \rvert$ | $\pm 1$ (sign of $\Delta x$) | $\Delta y / \lvert \Delta x \rvert$ ($+m$ right, $-m$ left) |
| 2 | $\lvert m \rvert = 1$, diagonal | $\lvert \Delta x \rvert = \lvert \Delta y \rvert$ | $\pm 1$ | $\pm 1$ |
| 3 | $\lvert m \rvert > 1$, steep | $\lvert \Delta y \rvert$ | $\Delta x / \lvert \Delta y \rvert$ ($+1/m$ up, $-1/m$ down) | $\pm 1$ (sign of $\Delta y$) |

Because $x_{\text{inc}} = \Delta x / \text{steps}$ and $y_{\text{inc}} = \Delta y / \text{steps}$ divide the signed coordinate differences by a positive step count, the increments automatically match the signs of $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$ in all directions.

## The algorithm

1. Compute coordinate differences $\Delta x = x_2 - x_1$ and $\Delta y = y_2 - y_1$.
2. Determine total steps: $\text{steps} = \max(\lvert\Delta x\rvert, \lvert\Delta y\rvert)$.
3. Compute coordinate increments: $x_{\text{inc}} = \Delta x / \text{steps}$ and $y_{\text{inc}} = \Delta y / \text{steps}$.
4. Initialize floating-point coordinates at the start point: $x = x_1$, $y = y_1$.
5. Loop $\text{steps} + 1$ iterations (the extra iteration plots the initial endpoint): illuminate pixel $(\text{round}(x), \text{round}(y))$, then update $x \leftarrow x + x_{\text{inc}}$ and $y \leftarrow y + y_{\text{inc}}$.

## Example 1: a gentle line, (5, 4) to (12, 7)

For endpoints $(5, 4)$ and $(12, 7)$, $\Delta x = 7$ and $\Delta y = 3$, giving $m = 3/7 \approx 0.429$. Because $\lvert m \rvert < 1$, this falls under Case 1: the algorithm executes 7 steps, incrementing $x$ by 1 and $y$ by $3/7 \approx 0.429$ per iteration:

| k | x | y (exact) | pixel |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 5 | 4 | (5, 4) |
| 1 | 6 | 4.429 | (6, 4) |
| 2 | 7 | 4.857 | (7, 5) |
| 3 | 8 | 5.286 | (8, 5) |
| 4 | 9 | 5.714 | (9, 6) |
| 5 | 10 | 6.143 | (10, 6) |
| 6 | 11 | 6.571 | (11, 7) |
| 7 | 12 | 7 | (12, 7) |

<Callout tone="orange" label="Cumulative error from rounding slope increments">
Rounding the slope to a coarse decimal like $0.4$ before executing the loop introduces cumulative error. With increment $0.4$, $y$ advances as $4.0, 4.4, 4.8, 5.2, 5.6, 6.0, 6.4, 6.8$, failing to reach the final endpoint value of $7.0$. At $x = 11$, the drifted sum $6.4$ rounds to pixel $(11, 6)$ rather than the correct $(11, 7)$. Each addition accumulates rounding error; always keep slope increments as exact fractions or full-precision floating-point values.
</Callout>

## Example 2: a steep line, (5, 7) to (10, 15)

For endpoints $(5, 7)$ and $(10, 15)$, $\Delta x = 5$ and $\Delta y = 8$, giving $m = 8/5 = 1.6$. Because $\lvert m \rvert > 1$, this falls under Case 3: $y$ is the dominant axis spanning 8 steps, while $x$ advances by $1/m = 5/8 = 0.625$ per step:

| k | x (exact) | y | pixel |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 5 | 7 | (5, 7) |
| 1 | 5.625 | 8 | (6, 8) |
| 2 | 6.25 | 9 | (6, 9) |
| 3 | 6.875 | 10 | (7, 10) |
| 4 | 7.5 | 11 | (8, 11) |
| 5 | 8.125 | 12 | (8, 12) |
| 6 | 8.75 | 13 | (9, 13) |
| 7 | 9.375 | 14 | (9, 14) |
| 8 | 10 | 15 | (10, 15) |

At iteration $k = 4$, $x = 7.5$ lies exactly equidistant between column 7 and column 8. Following the convention of rounding half values upward yields pixel $(8, 11)$.

## Example 3: diagonals, (3, 5) to (8, 10) and back

When $\Delta x = \Delta y = 5$ (Case 2), both coordinates advance by exactly 1 on every step without requiring fractional rounding: $(3, 5), (4, 6), (5, 7), (6, 8), (7, 9), (8, 10)$.

Reversing direction from $(8, 10)$ to $(3, 5)$ yields $\Delta x = \Delta y = -5$, so both increments evaluate to $-1$. The identical sequence of six pixels is plotted in reverse order, showing that the unified step equations handle every direction automatically.

## Example 4: horizontal, (2, 2) to (9, 2)

When $\Delta y = 0$, slope $m = 0$. The algorithm computes $\text{steps} = 7$, with $x_{\text{inc}} = 1$ and $y_{\text{inc}} = 0$, generating $(2, 2), (3, 2), \dots, (9, 2)$ (8 total pixels). For a vertical line ($\Delta x = 0$), $\text{steps} = \lvert\Delta y\rvert$ gives $x_{\text{inc}} = 0$ and $y_{\text{inc}} = \pm 1$ from the exact same formula, avoiding the naive algorithm's division by zero without a separate branch.

## Example 5: (2, 2) to (8, 5)

Some courses call this incremental procedure the "naive line-drawing algorithm" (computing $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$, setting $\text{steps} = \max(\lvert\Delta x\rvert, \lvert\Delta y\rvert)$, and repeatedly adding increments). In graphics textbooks, this is DDA.

Tracing from $(2, 2)$ to $(8, 5)$: $\Delta x = 6$, $\Delta y = 3$, giving 6 steps with increments $x_{\text{inc}} = 6/6 = 1.0$ and $y_{\text{inc}} = 3/6 = 0.5$.

| k | x | y (exact) | pixel |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 2 | 2 | (2, 2) |
| 1 | 3 | 2.5 | (3, 3) |
| 2 | 4 | 3 | (4, 3) |
| 3 | 5 | 3.5 | (5, 4) |
| 4 | 6 | 4 | (6, 4) |
| 5 | 7 | 4.5 | (7, 5) |
| 6 | 8 | 5 | (8, 5) |

<Callout tone="orange" label="Points vs discrete pixels">
Listing coordinates like $(3, 2.5)$ identifies mathematical locations along the continuous line, not pixel entries in a frame buffer. The final step in DDA is always rounding each calculated coordinate to its discrete pixel center. Rounding half values upward maps $(3, 2.5)$ to pixel $(3, 3)$.
</Callout>

## Limitations of DDA

While DDA eliminates gaps and replaces inner-loop multiplication with addition, it has notable limitations:

- It relies on [floating-point](/courses/logic-design/codes-and-floating-point#floating-point-very-large-and-very-small-numbers) arithmetic and requires an explicit rounding call per pixel, which consumes processor cycles.
- When rendering long line segments, repeated accumulation of floating-point inaccuracies causes calculated positions to gradually drift away from the true line.

<PracticalApplication title="Rasterization" tool="GPUs, game engines">
The principle of incremental coordinate evaluation remains ubiquitous in computer graphics. GPUs rasterize triangles by stepping edge coordinates incrementally across scan lines. Grid-traversal DDA variants are also standard in **ray casting** (shooting sight lines in games) to walk step by step across 3D block grids (**voxels**) or 2D tile maps.
</PracticalApplication>
