# Slope and the Line Equation

A **line-drawing algorithm** determines which pixels in a discrete grid to illuminate so that the resulting pattern best approximates a continuous straight line between two specified endpoints. Every raster line algorithm is derived from the mathematical equation of a line.

## Slope

A straight line has a constant rate of change called its **slope**, denoted by $m$. Slope measures vertical displacement (rise) divided by horizontal displacement (run) between two points $P_1(x_1, y_1)$ and $P_2(x_2, y_2)$:

$$
m = \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}
$$

The symbol $\Delta$ (delta) represents change. For example, given the endpoints $P_1(5, 10)$ and $P_2(11, 14)$:

$$
m = \frac{14 - 10}{11 - 5} = \frac{4}{6} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 0.667
$$

This line rises two-thirds of a pixel vertically for every single pixel it advances horizontally to the right.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Order of coordinates in slope calculation">
Slope is always the change in $y$ divided by the change in $x$, never the reverse. Furthermore, subtract the coordinates in consistent order in both numerator and denominator: $(14 - 10) / (11 - 5)$ and $(10 - 14) / (5 - 11)$ both correctly evaluate to $2/3$, but reversing the order in only one term, such as $(14 - 10) / (5 - 11)$, yields $-2/3$, inverting the slope.
</Callout>

## What the slope tells you

| Slope | Line behavior | Angle with x-axis |
|---|---|---|
| $m > 0$ | Rises from left to right | Between 0° and 90° |
| $m < 0$ | Falls from left to right | Between −90° and 0° (or 90° to 180° counterclockwise) |
| $m = 0$ | Purely horizontal ($\Delta y = 0$): $y$ remains constant | 0° |
| Undefined | Purely vertical ($\Delta x = 0$): division by zero | 90° |
| $\lvert m \rvert < 1$ | Gentle: horizontal run exceeds vertical rise | Magnitude less than 45° |
| $\lvert m \rvert = 1$ | Perfect diagonal | Magnitude 45° |
| $\lvert m \rvert > 1$ | Steep: vertical rise exceeds horizontal run | Magnitude greater than 45° |

The slope corresponds to the trigonometric tangent of the angle $\theta$ that the line forms with the positive x-axis: $m = \tan\theta$. A 45° diagonal line has a slope of $\tan 45° = 1$. For the example above, $\theta = \tan^{-1}(2/3) \approx 33.7°$, which is below 45° because its slope is less than 1.

<Callout tone="sky" label="The unit slope boundary">
The threshold $\lvert m \rvert = 1$ is critical for raster line algorithms. When $\lvert m \rvert > 1$, the line rises more than one row per column, so stepping only along $x$ skips rows and leaves gaps. Whether the absolute slope is below or above 1 decides which axis to step along.
</Callout>

## The slope-intercept equation

Any non-vertical straight line can be expressed in slope-intercept form:

$$
y = mx + c
$$

Here $m$ is the slope and $c$ is the **y-intercept**, the value of $y$ where the line crosses the y-axis (at $x = 0$). Given slope $m$ and endpoint $P_1(x_1, y_1)$, solve for $c$:

$$
c = y_1 - m x_1 = 10 - \frac{2}{3} \times 5 = 10 - 3.333 = 6.667
$$

The line passing through $P_1(5, 10)$ and $P_2(11, 14)$ is $y = \frac{2}{3}x + \frac{20}{3}$, or approximately $y = 0.667x + 6.667$. Evaluating the exact fraction at the second endpoint confirms it: $\frac{2}{3} \times 11 + \frac{20}{3} = \frac{42}{3} = 14$ (whereas the rounded decimals give $0.667 \times 11 + 6.667 = 14.004$, already showing small rounding drift).

## The naive algorithm: just use the equation

The most direct line-drawing approach evaluates this equation directly at each integer step:

1. Calculate slope $m$ and intercept $c$ from the given endpoints.
2. For each integer $x$ from $x_1$ to $x_2$, calculate continuous $y = mx + c$.
3. Because raster displays contain pixels only at discrete coordinates, **round** $y$ to the nearest integer and plot pixel $(x, \text{round}(y))$.

Step through the calculation for $P_1(5, 10)$ to $P_2(11, 14)$. The blue indicator shows the continuous position along the line, while the highlighted square marks the plotted pixel:

<Callout tone="orange" label="Do not round the intercept">
Rounding the intercept $c = 6.667$ to $7$ before plotting shifts the entire line upwards by a third of a pixel. Evaluating $y = 0.667x + 7$ yields pixels $(7, 12)$ and $(10, 14)$ instead of $(7, 11)$ and $(10, 13)$, and the continuous line no longer passes through $(5, 10)$ ($0.667 \times 5 + 7 = 10.335$, even though rounding still lands on pixel $(5, 10)$). Maintain full numerical precision for $m$ and $c$, and round only when converting the final $y$ coordinate to a pixel index.
</Callout>

### Rounding vs truncation

Some software implementations cast floating-point coordinates to integer types, which **truncates** rather than rounds: it discards the fractional portion, converting both 10.99 and 10.01 to 10. Truncating this sample line yields:

| x | exact y | rounded | truncated |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 10 | 10 | 10 |
| 6 | 10.667 | 11 | 10 |
| 7 | 11.333 | 11 | 11 |
| 8 | 12 | 12 | 12 |
| 9 | 12.667 | 13 | 12 |
| 10 | 13.333 | 13 | 13 |
| 11 | 14 | 14 | 14 |

Truncation consistently rounds downward, causing plotted pixels to deviate up to nearly a full pixel below the mathematical line. True rounding keeps every plotted pixel within half a pixel of the continuous line. In C and C++, `round(y)` or `static_cast<int>(y + 0.5)` rounds non-negative numbers to the nearest integer (rounding `.5` upward).

## Where the naive algorithm breaks

Evaluating $y = mx + c$ sequentially across $x$ has three drawbacks:

1. **Steep lines ($\lvert m \rvert > 1$)**: For a line spanning 9 rows ($\Delta y = 8$) across only 3 columns ($\Delta x = 2$), such as from $(2, 1)$ to $(4, 9)$, stepping along $x$ plots only 3 pixels. The resulting line has large vertical gaps between rows. To render steep lines without gaps, the algorithm must step along $y$ by single units and solve for $x = (y - c) / m$.
2. **Vertical lines**: When a line is purely vertical ($\Delta x = 0$), the slope $m = \Delta y / \Delta x$ requires division by zero, making the formulation undefined.
3. **Computational overhead**: Every pixel calculation requires a [floating-point](/courses/logic-design/codes-and-floating-point#floating-point-very-large-and-very-small-numbers) multiplication ($m \cdot x$), a floating-point addition, and a rounding operation. On graphics hardware rendering thousands of lines per frame, repetitive floating-point multiplication creates a performance bottleneck.
