# How Much Memory an Image Needs

Every pixel in an uncompressed image occupies a fixed number of bits, so finding the total memory requirement is a straightforward calculation. This is a common exercise in computer graphics courses. Following the same sequence of steps every time avoids common unit errors.

## The method

1. **Calculate total pixels:** width × height.
2. **Calculate total bits:** pixels × bits per pixel.
3. **Convert to bytes:** divide by 8.
4. **Convert to kilobytes:** divide by 1024.
5. **Convert to megabytes:** divide by 1024 again.

Why divide by 1024 instead of 1000? Computer operating systems and hardware measure memory capacities using [powers of two](/courses/computer-organization/bits-and-bytes#how-much-is-a-lot), where $2^{10} = 1024$ bytes equal one binary kilobyte (KB). Use 1024 for RAM and frame buffer sizes unless a specification explicitly states decimal units (1000).

<Callout tone="orange" label="Two classic slips">
Skipping step 3 leaves the answer in bits rather than bytes, making the result 8 times too large. In addition, step 2 multiplies by the **bit depth** (such as 24 for true color), never by the count of distinct colors ($16{,}777{,}216$).
</Callout>

When bit depth is a multiple of 8, steps 2 and 3 can be combined into a single step. For a 24-bit image, multiplying pixels directly by 3 yields bytes ($24 \div 8 = 3$). This shortcut works for 8-bit (1 byte), 16-bit (2 bytes), 24-bit (3 bytes), and 32-bit (4 bytes) formats, but does not apply to sub-byte formats like 1-bit or 4-bit images.

## Worked example: one image, five depths

Consider an image measuring 640 × 480 pixels. It contains $640 \times 480 = 307{,}200$ pixels.

**1-bit (binary):**

$$
\begin{aligned}
307{,}200 \text{ bits} \div 8 &= 38{,}400 \text{ bytes} \\
38{,}400 \div 1024 &= 37.5 \text{ KB}
\end{aligned}
$$

**8-bit (grayscale):** one byte per pixel.

$$
307{,}200 \text{ bytes} \div 1024 = 300 \text{ KB}
$$

**16-bit (high color):** two bytes per pixel.

$$
\begin{aligned}
307{,}200 \times 2 &= 614{,}400 \text{ bytes} \\
614{,}400 \div 1024 &= 600 \text{ KB}
\end{aligned}
$$

**24-bit (true color):** three bytes per pixel.

$$
\begin{aligned}
307{,}200 \times 3 &= 921{,}600 \text{ bytes} \\
921{,}600 \div 1024 &= 900 \text{ KB}
\end{aligned}
$$

**32-bit (true color with alpha):** four bytes per pixel.

$$
\begin{aligned}
307{,}200 \times 4 &= 1{,}228{,}800 \text{ bytes} \\
1{,}228{,}800 \div 1024 &= 1200 \text{ KB} \\
1200 \div 1024 &\approx 1.17 \text{ MB}
\end{aligned}
$$

The results show a clear relationship: the 8-bit image is 8 times the size of the 1-bit image, and the 24-bit image is 3 times the size of the 8-bit image. Uncompressed storage requirements grow in direct proportion to bit depth.

## Worked example: a camera photo

A photograph 1200 pixels wide and 800 pixels high, in 24-bit true color:

$$
\begin{aligned}
1200 \times 800 &= 960{,}000 \text{ pixels} \\
960{,}000 \times 3 &= 2{,}880{,}000 \text{ bytes} \\
2{,}880{,}000 \div 1024 &= 2812.5 \text{ KB} \\
2812.5 \div 1024 &\approx 2.75 \text{ MB}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now calculate the memory needed for a large 14.7 megapixel image measuring 4700 × 3133 in true color:

$$
\begin{aligned}
4700 \times 3133 &= 14{,}725{,}100 \text{ pixels} \\
14{,}725{,}100 \times 3 &= 44{,}175{,}300 \text{ bytes} \\
44{,}175{,}300 \div 1024 &\approx 43{,}139.94 \text{ KB} \\
43{,}139.94 \div 1024 &\approx 42.13 \text{ MB}
\end{aligned}
$$

<Callout tone="orange" label="Round at the end">
Maintain full numerical precision throughout intermediate steps and round only at the very end. For example, $44{,}175{,}300 \div 1024^2 \approx 42.1288\text{ MB}$ rounds to 42.13 MB; truncating digits early produces 42.12 MB instead.
</Callout>

## Uncompressed memory versus file compression

These calculations determine the memory an image occupies in RAM or within a display [frame buffer](/courses/computer-graphics/crt-and-raster-scan#the-frame-buffer) while being displayed or edited, as well as file sizes in uncompressed formats like BMP. Image files stored on disk as JPEG or PNG are usually much smaller because they compress redundant pixel data. However, display hardware cannot read compressed formats directly onto the screen: before an image is displayed, software or hardware must decode it into an uncompressed pixel grid in memory.
