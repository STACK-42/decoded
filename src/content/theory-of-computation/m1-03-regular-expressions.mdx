# Reading and Writing Regular Expressions

[The Language of Languages](/courses/theory-of-computation/language-of-languages#regular-expressions-the-first-compact-notation) defined regular expressions with three rules: union, concatenation, and star. Exams ask three questions about them:

1. **Describe the language** of a given expression in words.
2. **List the first words** of its language.
3. **Write an expression** for a language described in words.

## The notation, and what binds tighter

A **regular expression** is a short formula for a language, built from single symbols and the empty string with three operations: union, concatenation and star.

Course notes and exams use textbook notation, which differs from the regex in your editor in one important way: **$+$ means "or"**. In `grep` or JavaScript, `+` means "one or more", but here $a + b$ is the language $\{a, b\}$.

| Notation | Means | Example | Language |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $r_1 + r_2$ | union: $r_1$ or $r_2$ | $a + b$ | $\{a, b\}$ |
| $r_1 r_2$ | concatenation: $r_1$ then $r_2$ | $ab$ | $\{ab\}$ |
| $r^*$ | zero or more repetitions of $r$ | $a^*$ | $\{\varepsilon, a, aa, \ldots\}$ |
| $\varepsilon$ (or $\lambda$, $\Lambda$) | the empty string | $a + \varepsilon$ | $\{\varepsilon, a\}$ |

Star binds tightest, then concatenation, then union. So $ab^*$ means $a(b^*)$, which is one $a$ followed by any number of $b$'s ($\{a, ab, abb, \ldots\}$), while $(ab)^*$ repeats the whole pair ($\{\varepsilon, ab, abab, \ldots\}$). Likewise, $a + bc$ means $a + (bc) = \{a, bc\}$, whereas $(a + b)c = \{ac, bc\}$. When in doubt, add brackets.

## Reading an expression

Read from the outside in. Find the top-level operator (the last operation applied, usually union `+` outside parentheses, or concatenation if there is no unparenthesized `+`), say what each part contributes, then combine. Four examples:

- **$a^* + b^*$:** The top-level operator is `+` (union), joining $a^*$ and $b^*$. Every word comes entirely from $a^*$ or entirely from $b^*$: "only $a$'s, or only $b$'s" ($\varepsilon$, $a$, $b$, $aa$, $bb$, and so on). Both halves allow zero repetitions, so $\varepsilon$ is in the language, while a word like $ab$ mixes the two and is **not** in the language.
- **$a^*ab^*$:** There is no top-level `+`, so the expression is a concatenation of three pieces: $a^*$, then $a$, then $b^*$. Because $a^*$ can contribute $\varepsilon$, the shortest word is $a$. The required middle $a$ guarantees at least one $a$, followed by zero or more $b$'s: $a$, $aa$, $ab$, $aaa$, $aab$, $abb$, and so on (in tool syntax, `a+b*`). Every $a$ must appear before every $b$, so $ba$ is not in the language.
- **$(a + b)^*ba(a + b)^*$:** Since $(a + b)^*$ matches any string over $\{a, b\}$ (the [Kleene star](/courses/theory-of-computation/language-of-languages#kleene-star-sigma) of the alphabet), this concatenation reads as "anything, then $ba$, then anything": every word that **contains** $ba$ somewhere.
- **$ab^*ab^*a$:** Concatenation of five pieces ($a$, $b^*$, $a$, $b^*$, $a$). That fixes three $a$'s (at the start, somewhere in the middle, and at the end) with zero or more $b$'s in the two gaps: every word over $\{a, b\}$ that starts with $a$, ends with $a$, and has **exactly three** $a$'s.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Two reading traps: a* + b* and (a + b)*">
  Students often confuse $a^* + b^*$ with $(a + b)^*$. In $a^* + b^*$, the choice between $a$ and $b$ happens **once** at the start, so you never switch letters ($ab$ is rejected). In $(a + b)^*$, the choice happens on **every step**, so any mix of $a$'s and $b$'s is allowed.
</Callout>

## Listing the first words

When a question asks for "the first 10 words", list them **shortest first, and alphabetically within the same length** (often called **shortlex order** or **length-lexicographical order**). If the empty word $\varepsilon$ is in the language, it always comes first because it has length $0$. To generate words systematically, pick the shortest option at every part of the expression, then increase the repetition count one step at a time.

Walk through $(ab + ba)^*aa$ step by step:
1. **0 repetitions** of $(ab + ba)$: $\varepsilon \cdot aa = aa$ (1 word of length 2).
2. **1 repetition** of $(ab + ba)$: choose either $ab$ or $ba$, then append $aa$, giving $abaa$ and $baaa$ (2 words of length 4, already in alphabetical order).
3. **2 repetitions** of $(ab + ba)$: choose from $\{ab, ba\}$ twice, then append $aa$, giving four combinations: $ababaa$, $abbaaa$, $baabaa$, and $babaaa$ (4 words of length 6, sorted alphabetically).

That puts the first seven words in exact order: $aa$, $abaa$, $baaa$, $ababaa$, $abbaaa$, $baabaa$, $babaaa$. Check that against the tool below, then try the other exercise expressions: `ab*ab*a`, `(ab)*+(ba)*`, `bb(a+b)*aa`, `(ab+ba)*(bb+aa)`.

## Writing an expression

Most exam languages are built from a handful of patterns over $\Sigma = \{a, b\}$:

| The language | Pattern |
| --- | --- |
| Words that **end** with $w$ | $(a + b)^*w$ |
| Words that **start** with $w$ | $w(a + b)^*$ |
| Words that **contain** $w$ | $(a + b)^*w(a + b)^*$ |
| Words with **exactly one** $a$ | $b^*ab^*$ |
| Words of **even length** | $((a + b)(a + b))^*$ |

Notice why "exactly one $a$" uses $b^*ab^*$ while "contains $a$" uses $(a + b)^*a(a + b)^*$: around the fixed $a$, $b^*$ allows only $b$'s, blocking any second $a$ from sneaking into the gaps.

When writing an expression from a word description, work in three steps:
1. **Split into cases** if several shapes are allowed, and join them with $+$.
2. **Place the required parts** in order, and fill each gap with $(a + b)^*$ if anything is allowed there, or with only the non-forbidden letters (like $b^*$) if a symbol is banned.
3. **Test from both sides:** list a few short words your expression generates and verify each belongs, then think of edge-case words (especially $\varepsilon$ and single-letter words) and verify your expression handles them. The checker below does the second half for you by trying every word up to length 8 and reporting the shortest one where your answer and the language disagree.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Check the empty word and 'exactly' vs 'at least'">
  Most wrong answers fail on the shortest words or on gaps between required letters:
  - **Check $\varepsilon$ first:** "No double letters" includes $\varepsilon$; "ends with $bbb$" does not.
  - **"Exactly" vs "at least":** Writing $(a+b)^*a(a+b)^*a(a+b)^*$ for "exactly two $a$'s" is wrong because $(a+b)^*$ lets extra $a$'s into the gaps, matching *at least* two $a$'s instead. To lock in an exact count, use $b^*$ in the gaps ($b^*ab^*ab^*$).
</Callout>

## Where this shows up today

<ApplicationsGrid>
  <PracticalApplication title="Translating to tool syntax" tool="GREP">
    Textbook union $+$ is `|` in tools, so $(a + b)^*bbb$ becomes `(a|b)*bbb`. Tools add shorthands like `a+` for $aa^*$ and `a?` for $(a + \varepsilon)$, but every one of them can be rewritten with the three textbook operators.
  </PracticalApplication>
  <PracticalApplication title="Input validation" tool="WEB">
    Patterns in HTML `pattern` attributes and validation libraries are regular expressions. Writing them with the "starts with / contains / exactly one" patterns above, then testing the [empty string](/courses/theory-of-computation/language-of-languages#string-word) first, catches most bugs before users do.
  </PracticalApplication>
</ApplicationsGrid>
