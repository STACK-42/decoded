# The Physical Layer

<Epigraph quote="Computer literacy is a contact with the activity of computing deep enough to make the computational equivalent of reading and writing fluent and enjoyable." by="Alan Kay" source="Computer Software, Scientific American, 1984" />

When you use a computer, every task is a loop: you send an instruction, and the machine responds. A person listening to you can guess what you meant from context. A computer cannot. It only reacts to **structured input**: exact signals such as keypresses, mouse clicks, file paths, search queries, or typed commands. Because the machine works only with what you give it, the clarity and speed of your input decide what you get back.

Most people never stop to look at that loop. They treat computers as tools that sometimes cooperate and sometimes fight back, without noticing how much **fluency** matters. Fluency means expressing what you want smoothly, without stopping to fight the keyboard or mouse. Each layer of fluency removes a different kind of slowdown: first the **physical layer** (how your hands use the hardware), then the [language layer](/courses/computer-literacy/language-layer) (how you tell software what you want), and finally the [systems layer](/courses/computer-literacy/systems-layer) (how you read what the machine is doing underneath).

To see what the machine records with each keypress, [Letters are numbers](/courses/computer-organization/bits-and-bytes#letters-are-numbers) shows how characters are stored as binary values.

## Typing

Typing is the main part of the physical layer. Most beginners rely on **hunt-and-peck** typing: looking down at the keyboard to spot each key and pressing it with one or two fingers. The alternative is **touch typing**, where all ten fingers stay on assigned keys and press them by muscle memory (remembering key positions by feel) without looking down.

Typing speed is measured in **words per minute (WPM)**, where one standard "word" is five characters (including spaces and punctuation) and uncorrected errors lower your score. When you type slowly by looking at the keys, searching interrupts your **working memory**, the small mental scratchpad that holds only a few pieces of an idea at once. Trace what happens when you try to write a single line of code like `total = price * count` at 25 WPM:

1. You hold the full expression in your head.
2. You look down to find `=` and `*`, taking your eyes off the screen.
3. When you look back up, you have to re-read the line to remember what you meant to write next.

At around 60 WPM, your hands roughly keep up with normal thinking, so you can write while you form the thought. Around 90 WPM, the keyboard stops slowing you down. Saving a few seconds is only part of it. The bigger gain is that you stop spending attention hunting for keys, so your focus stays on the idea.

<Callout tone="neon" label="The test">
You are fluent enough when you can finish typing a sentence whose end you had not yet planned when you started.
</Callout>

### Building the muscle

Touch typing starts from the **home row**: the middle row of letter keys (`A S D F` for your left hand, `J K L ;` for your right hand) where your fingers rest between keypresses. Feel the small raised bumps on the `F` and `J` keys. Those bumps exist so your index fingers can find the home row by touch alone; every other key is one row up, one row down, or one key sideways from a resting finger.

To train it, put your index fingers on `F` and `J`, aim for accuracy first so each finger learns its reach, keep your eyes off the keyboard, and let speed come later. Your typing speed will drop for about two weeks while old habits break, and then the slowness goes away.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Usual mistake: chasing speed and peeking">
Two habits slow beginners down. First, pushing for high WPM while making constant typos forces you to hit Backspace and trains your fingers to repeat the wrong motion. Clean accuracy at 30 WPM builds real speed faster than sloppy bursts at 50. Second, glancing down whenever you forget a symbol like `{` or `=` keeps your fingers from learning where that key sits, which can turn a two-week change into months.
</Callout>

## Shortcuts

A **keyboard shortcut** (also called a **hotkey** or **keybinding**) is a key combination, usually a **modifier key** such as `Ctrl`, `Cmd`, or `Alt` held down with a letter or symbol, that triggers an action directly without touching the mouse. I once had a week when my mouse kept lagging, so I had to do everything from the keyboard. It showed me that you can run almost any program without leaving the keyboard, and once you see that, you stop reaching for the mouse.

Compare copying a line from a web page into a text editor step by step:

- **With the mouse:** lift your right hand off the home row, find the pointer, drag across the line, right-click, click *Copy*, move to the other window, click the target line, right-click, click *Paste*, and move your hand back to the keyboard.
- **With shortcuts:** keep both hands on the keyboard, press `Ctrl+C` (`Cmd+C` on macOS) to copy, `Alt+Tab` (`Cmd+Tab`) to switch windows, and `Ctrl+V` (`Cmd+V`) to paste.

A few everyday shortcuts remove dozens of these breaks per hour: `Ctrl+L` (`Cmd+L`) jumps straight to a browser's address bar, `Ctrl+F` (`Cmd+F`) searches inside almost any window, and `Ctrl+Shift+T` (`Cmd+Shift+T`) reopens a tab you closed by accident. People who use shortcuts work faster, and they think more clearly while they work because their tools stop interrupting the thought.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Usual mistake: memorizing a full list at once">
Printing a list of 40 shortcuts and trying to memorize them in one sitting rarely works. Wait until you catch yourself reaching for the mouse for the same action three times in one day, look up the single shortcut for it, and use it until it feels automatic before adding the next.
</Callout>

## Practice by hand

**1. Calculate WPM from characters:** One standard word is five characters, including spaces and punctuation. Suppose you type 300 characters in 1 minute and 30 seconds (1.5 minutes) with zero uncorrected errors. How many standard words did you type, and what is your WPM? Then suppose on a second run you type 200 characters in 1 minute, leaving 5 uncorrected errors behind: is your net speed still 40 WPM?

<ModelAnswer>
- **First run:** Divide characters by 5 to get standard words: $300 \div 5 = 60$ words. Divide by 1.5 minutes: $60 \div 1.5 = 40\text{ WPM}$.
- **Second run:** $200 \div 5 = 40$ raw words in 1 minute, so the raw speed is 40 WPM, but uncorrected errors lower your score below 40 WPM. Pushing faster while leaving errors trains your fingers to repeat mistakes and forces constant Backspace corrections later.
</ModelAnswer>

**2. Replace repeated mouse trips with shortcuts:** You are reading documentation in a browser, copying a line into a text editor, switching back to the browser, and reopening a tab you closed by accident. Which shortcuts replace those mouse actions, and how should you build the habit?

<ModelAnswer>
- Use `Ctrl+C` (`Cmd+C` on macOS) to copy, `Alt+Tab` (`Cmd+Tab`) to switch between the browser and editor, `Ctrl+V` (`Cmd+V`) to paste, and `Ctrl+Shift+T` (`Cmd+Shift+T`) to reopen the closed tab. (`Ctrl+L` or `Cmd+L` also jumps straight to the browser address bar when you need a new URL.)
- Build the habit one shortcut at a time: notice when you reach for the mouse three times in one day for the same task, learn that single shortcut, and repeat it until it feels automatic before adding another.
</ModelAnswer>
