# Streamify, Stage 1: Requirements and Quality Scenarios

Lectures 1 to 5 gave you the pieces: what architecture is, design principles, views and styles. Lecture 6 puts them together on one large system. The system is a Spotify-style music streaming platform, rebuilt here under the name **Streamify**.

The lecture's guiding question is: *which style fits each subsystem, given its most important quality attributes?* It warns from the start that **a single style for the whole system is the wrong answer.**

## Business context and drivers

The vision behind Streamify:

> "Give users instant access to any song, on any device, anywhere, at the moment they want it, and pay artists fairly for each play."

The vision breaks into five **<Term id="business-driver">business drivers</Term>**:

| ID | Business driver | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| BD1 | Instant satisfaction | Playback must feel like a local file. This was the main advantage over piracy. |
| BD2 | Global scale | Hundreds of millions of users in many markets. |
| BD3 | Licensing compliance | Rights differ per country, and every play must be counted. |
| BD4 | Personalisation | Recommendations (such as a weekly personal playlist) keep users engaged. |
| BD5 | Freemium monetisation | A free tier paid for by ads, plus paid subscriptions. |

## Stakeholders and their concerns

| Stakeholder | Main concerns |
| --- | --- |
| Listeners (free and premium) | Fast start, no stutter, offline play, good recommendations |
| Artists and distributors | Correct royalty reports, control of their catalog, analytics |
| Advertisers | Ad targeting, reports of how often ads were heard |
| Product and data teams | Experiments (A/B tests), good-quality event data |
| Operations / Site Reliability Engineers (SRE) | Availability, observability, cost per stream |
| Legal and licensing | Geo-restriction, <Term id="drm">DRM</Term> |
| Developers | Deploying their part independently, team autonomy |

These concerns conflict. Listeners want instant play, legal wants DRM checks first, and SRE wants a lower cost per stream. The architecture has to balance them.

## Functional requirements

A core subset, prioritised with <Term id="moscow">MoSCoW</Term> ("shall" = must, "could" = could):

| ID | Requirement |
| --- | --- |
| FR1 | Users shall be able to register, log in and manage a subscription (free or premium). |
| FR2 | Users shall be able to search the catalog by track, artist, album, playlist or podcast. |
| FR3 | Users shall be able to stream a track on demand, with seek, skip and pause. |
| FR4 | Users shall be able to create, edit, share and follow playlists. |
| FR5 | Premium users shall be able to download tracks for offline play (DRM-protected). |
| FR6 | The system could recommend content (home feed, weekly personal playlists). |
| FR7 | Distributors shall be able to submit new audio and metadata. |
| FR8 | The system shall record every play, for royalties and analytics. |
| FR9 | Free-tier users shall hear targeted audio ads between tracks. |
| FR10 | Users could continue playback across devices (the "Connect" handoff). |

Watch FR3, FR5, FR7 and FR8. They look like simple features, but each one leads to a different architectural style later: client-server and peer-to-peer for streaming, pipe-and-filter for ingestion, messaging for recording plays.

## Quality-attribute scenarios: six parts

"The system should be fast" can't be tested, and you can't use it to compare two designs. A **<Term id="qa-scenario">quality-attribute scenario</Term>** fixes that by writing every quality requirement in six parts:

| Part | QA1, play latency |
| --- | --- |
| Source | a listener |
| Stimulus | presses play on a track |
| Artifact | the playback path (client + audio delivery) |
| Environment | the track is not in the local cache; normal load |
| Response | playback starts |
| **<Term id="response-measure">Response measure</Term>** | **≤ 250 ms median, ≤ 1 s <Term id="p99">p99</Term>** |

Written out: *"On a cache miss under normal load, playback starts in ≤ 250 ms (median), ≤ 1 s (p99)."* Now you can measure it, test it, and trade it against other qualities. The median says what a typical play feels like; the p99 says how bad the slow ones get.

Streamify's eight scenarios:

| ID | Quality | Scenario | Response measure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| QA1 | Performance (latency) | play a track not in the local cache, normal load | starts in ≤ 250 ms median, ≤ 1 s p99 |
| QA2 | Performance (continuity) | a track plays over a variable mobile network | stutter (buffer underrun) in < 1 % of plays |
| QA3 | Scalability | concurrent streams grow from 5 M to 15 M at peak (e.g. New Year's Eve) | QA1 still holds; scales out without a redesign |
| QA4 | Availability | a whole data-centre region fails | cached and CDN playback continues; core APIs back in ≤ 5 min; playback ≥ 99.95 % per year |
| QA5 | Reliability (data) | a client sends a play event while offline or during an outage | delivered at least once and de-duplicated; no important event lost |
| QA6 | Modifiability | a team changes the recommendation algorithm | deployed on its own, without cross-team coordination, in < 1 day |
| QA7 | Security | an attacker tries to extract raw audio from the client | audio encrypted at rest and in transit; keys bound to the device and user |
| QA8 | Cost efficiency | bandwidth demand doubles | cost per stream-hour grows less than linearly (caching, CDN, compression) |

Three of them already point at later decisions:

- **QA4** means playback must survive even when the backend APIs are down, so the client and the <Term id="cdn">CDN</Term> must keep working on their own.
- **QA5** rules out "fire-and-forget" telemetry. Events need durable, buffered, replayable transport, which leads to [messaging](/courses/advanced-software-engineering/pipe-and-filter-and-messaging#messaging).
- **QA8**, together with constraint C5 below, is why Spotify first used listeners' own computers to deliver music: [peer-to-peer](/courses/advanced-software-engineering/n-tier-client-server-and-peer-to-peer#peer-to-peer).

## Constraints

A **<Term id="constraint">constraint</Term>** is a limit the design must stay inside. You don't trade constraints off against other qualities; you design within them.

| ID | Constraint | Architectural consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C1 | Licensing: contracts require DRM and per-country availability rules | a key service, encrypted audio, geo-rules in the catalog (QA7) |
| C2 | Many kinds of client: iOS, Android, desktop, web, speakers, cars, consoles, TVs | a gateway / <Term id="bff">Backend-for-Frontend</Term>, a shared client core (layering) |
| C3 | Mobile limits: battery, data plans, frequent loss of connection | local cache, buffering, events buffered on the device (QA2, QA5) |
| C4 | Regulation: GDPR and similar personal-data laws | each service owns its data; controlled access to the data platform |
| C5 | In 2008: CDN bandwidth was expensive, and the start-up had little money | offload delivery to listeners' devices → hybrid P2P (QA8) |

C5 is tied to its time. When CDN costs fell and phones took over, C5 stopped applying, and the P2P design was retired. [Stage 4](/courses/advanced-software-engineering/choosing-styles-per-subsystem#style-3-peer-to-peer-historic-2008-to-2014) tells that story.

## Back-of-the-envelope estimation

A **<Term id="back-of-the-envelope">back-of-the-envelope estimate</Term>** is a rough calculation, right to about the nearest power of ten. Its job is to show which problems are *unusually big* for this system, because those are what drive the choice of style.

| Quantity | Assumption | Calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Daily active users | 100 M (given) | | 100 M DAU |
| Peak concurrent streams | ~15 % of DAU | 100 M × 0.15 | 15 M streams |
| Average bitrate | Ogg / AAC (given) | | 160 kbps |
| Peak <Term id="egress">egress</Term> bandwidth | all peak streams | 15 M × 160 kbps | ≈ 2.4 Tbps |
| Catalog storage | 100 M tracks × 4 encodings × ~5 MB | 2 × 10⁹ MB | ≈ 2 PB |
| Event volume | ~200 events per user per day | 100 M × 200 = 20 B/day ÷ 86 400 s | ≈ 230 K/s average, ~700 K/s peak |

### From numbers to decisions

| Number | What kind of problem? | Architectural response | Style |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 2.4 Tbps peak egress | a bandwidth problem, not a compute problem | local caching, a CDN, and in the early years listeners' devices | client-server + CDN; historically P2P |
| ≈ 700 K events/s peak | a throughput problem: synchronous request/response would overload the backend | buffer, batch and send events asynchronously | messaging (pub/sub) |
| ≈ 2 PB, mostly audio that never changes | a storage and distribution problem | blob/object storage, caching by content | supports every delivery style |

## Prioritising: the utility tree

Not every requirement drives the architecture. **[Architecturally significant requirements](/courses/advanced-software-engineering/architecture-process-and-styles#step-1-determine-architectural-requirements)** (ASRs) are the ones that do. Streamify ranks its scenarios with a **<Term id="utility-tree">utility tree</Term>**, a tool from <Term id="atam">ATAM</Term>: each scenario gets a rating for business importance and for technical difficulty, High, Medium or Low.

| Rating (importance, difficulty) | Scenario | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| (H, H) | QA1, QA3, QA5, QA6, plus constraint C1 | the hardest and most valuable: design these first. **These are Streamify's ASRs.** |
| (H, L) | QA7 security | critical, but solved with known techniques |
| (M, H) | QA8 cost | hard, but negotiable |

Treating everything as top priority means nothing is prioritised.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common mistake">
Writing quality requirements like "fast" or "scalable". They can't be tested, so they can't steer a design. Write the six parts, with a number in the response measure. A second trap from the estimates: "add more servers" doesn't solve a 2.4 Tbps egress problem. First ask which resource is the bottleneck.
</Callout>
