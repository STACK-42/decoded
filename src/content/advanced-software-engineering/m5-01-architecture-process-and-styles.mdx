# The Architecture Process and Architectural Styles

Lecture 5 moves from *describing* an architecture to *building* one. It starts with vocabulary, then walks through the steps an architect follows.

## Terminology review: views and styles

**What is an architectural view?** Views reflect three broad ways an architect looks at a system (from [Lecture 4](/courses/advanced-software-engineering/views-and-structures#three-kinds-of-structures)):

- units of implementation → **module views**,
- runtime units → **C&C views**,
- relation to non-software structures → **allocation views**.

**Architectural styles vs. architectural views.** Choosing a view still leaves many choices open:

- what kinds of **relationships** are allowed?
- what kinds of **constraints** are enforced?

**Different choices lead to different <Term id="architectural-style">architectural styles</Term>.** So a style can describe modules, runtime units, or software and non-software elements, depending on the kind of view it lives in. A style is a reusable, named package of such choices.

## Basic properties of a style

Every style defines three things:

1. **A vocabulary of design elements:** the <Term id="component">component</Term> and connector types and data elements it uses. *E.g. pipes, filters, objects, servers.*
2. **A set of configuration rules:** <Term id="topology">topological</Term> constraints that decide which compositions of elements are allowed. *E.g. a component may be connected to at most two other components.*
3. **A semantic interpretation:** compositions of design elements have well-defined meanings. *E.g. in pipe-and-filter, an arrow always means "this data stream flows here."*

## The role of the software architect

The architect's job is to:

- **work with the requirements team**: the explicit requirements;
- **work with the application's stakeholders**: to find out *why* (the qualities that matter to them, which are often unstated);
- **lead the technical design team**;
- **work with the project team** (planning, schedule, staffing).

## The architecture process

The process has three steps, and it's **iterative**: validation sends you back to revise the requirements or the design.

### Step 1: determine architectural requirements

Architecture requirements, also called **<Term id="architecturally-significant-requirements">architecturally significant requirements</Term>** or **architecture use cases**, are essentially the quality and <Term id="non-functional-requirement">non-functional requirements</Term> of the application. They come from two sources: the **functional requirements** and the **stakeholder requirements**.

*Example:* "show the account balance" is a functional requirement. "Show it within 1 second for 10,000 simultaneous users" is architecturally significant: it decides caching, replication and the choice of style.

### Step 2: architecture design

Its **input** is the architecture requirements; its **outputs** are the **architecture views** and the **architecture document**. In between are two sub-steps:

- **Step 2a, choose the architecture framework:** choose an overall strategy, based on proven **architecture patterns** (a.k.a. **architectural styles**).
- **Step 2b, allocate components:** specify the individual components that make up the application, show how they fit into the overall framework, and assign them responsibilities.

### Step 2a in practice

- **Reusing known solutions lowers risk:** an established style reduces the chance that the application fails because of an unsuitable architecture.
- **Patterns have clear pros and cons** with respect to specific <Term id="quality-attribute">quality attributes</Term>. That's what makes them useful: you pick a style *because of* the qualities you need.
- **Small applications:** one pattern, like n-tier client-server, may be enough.
- **Complex applications:** the design combines several known patterns, and the architect specifies how they fit together into the whole.

The styles the lecture covers:

- **Layered architecture (n-tier)**, including client-server
- **Peer-to-peer**
- **Pipe-and-filter**
- **Messaging**
- **Implicit invocation**, including publish-subscribe (e.g. Spotify) and event-based (e.g. the Eclipse IDE)
- **Web services**
- **Microservices**
- also: traditional language-influenced styles (main program and subroutines; object-oriented/abstract data types) and **shared data**

<Callout tone="mint" label="Connection">
Choosing a style is applying the [First Law](/courses/advanced-software-engineering/the-architects-role#the-laws-of-software-architecture): each style buys some qualities by giving up others. Recording why you chose it is the Second Law.
</Callout>

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common mistake">
Confusing a style with a view. A view is a kind of picture (module, C&C, allocation); a style is a reusable set of choices made *within* a view. Another mistake: driving the architecture from features alone. Quality requirements are what make a requirement architecturally significant.
</Callout>
