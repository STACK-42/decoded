# The Architect's Role and the Laws of Architecture

What is an architecture made of, what does an architect actually do, and what rules hold for every architecture?

## The 4D model of architecture

Architecture is more than boxes and lines. The lecture describes it in four dimensions:

| Dimension | What it is | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. **Structure** | the architectural style | microservices, event-driven, microkernel |
| 2. **<Term id="architecture-characteristics">Architecture characteristics</Term>** | the "-ilities" the system must have | scalability, availability, security |
| 3. **Architecture decisions** | hard rules the system must follow | "The UI cannot query the database directly." |
| 4. **Design principles** | guidelines that point a direction, not hard rules | "Prefer asynchronous messaging." |

The difference between 3 and 4: a decision is a constraint you must not break; a principle is advice you follow unless there's a good reason not to.

## The eight expectations of an architect

**Technical expectations**

1. **Make architecture decisions.** Set the structural boundaries and rules, rather than micromanaging which specific tools each team uses.
2. **Continually analyze.** Keep checking whether the architecture is still healthy, to stop structural decay early.
3. **Keep current with trends.** Watch for technical shifts and prepare for them.
4. **Ensure compliance.** Set up automated governance that checks the rules are followed, using <Term id="fitness-function">fitness functions</Term>.

**Leadership, breadth and politics**

5. **Diverse exposure.** Build wide, varied technical breadth rather than deep expertise in one area.
6. **Business domain knowledge.** Understand the domain's concepts, goals, vocabulary and market.
7. **Interpersonal skills.** Lead, run discussions, coach and mentor. Work as a peer, not a boss.
8. **Navigate politics.** Defend trade-offs objectively when stakeholders push back.

<Callout tone="orange" label="The compliance imperative">
A whiteboard drawing that nobody follows is not architecture; it's modern art. Making sure the architecture is actually followed is the architect's first job, and fitness functions are how you automate that.
</Callout>

## Two case studies: architecture depends on context

**Pets.com: the failure of elastic scale.** The company launched a huge national marketing campaign, which caused a surge of simultaneous orders. It had put business behavior (marketing, checkout features) ahead of operational architecture. Without <Term id="elastic-scale">elastic scale</Term>, the databases locked, transactions were corrupted, order fulfilment choked, and the business was liquidated.

*Takeaway:* scale is not a feature you add later. It is a basic characteristic that has to be designed into the structure.

**A data center in 2002: the economics of styles.** In 2002, running 50 isolated services meant paying millions for commercial server and database licenses and hardware racks. Today, containers (Docker, Kubernetes) make starting another isolated service almost free.

*Takeaway:* architectural styles are products of their historical and economic context. What was reckless in 2002 is industry standard today.

## Technical depth versus technical breadth

As a developer you spent years building <Term id="technical-depth">technical depth</Term>: expertise in one stack. An architect must give up some depth to widen <Term id="technical-breadth">technical breadth</Term>.

## Process versus engineering practices

Students and organizations often mix these up:

| | Software development process | Software engineering practices |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it governs | who does the work and when | how the code is built |
| Examples | Scrum, Kanban: meetings, cards, sprint retrospectives | continuous integration, TDD, automated linters |
| Nature | human-centric workflow | technical, objective, works with any process |

**The architectural axiom:** a process framework like Scrum can't repair or protect a decoupled architecture. Only disciplined engineering practices, such as fitness functions, can.

## The laws of software architecture

**First Law: "Everything in software architecture is a <Term id="trade-off">trade-off</Term>."**

*Corollary:* if you think you've found an architectural pattern without a big trade-off, you just haven't found the trade-off yet.

*Example:* <Term id="microservices">microservices</Term> give independent scaling, but bring network <Term id="latency">latency</Term>, failures in transactions that span services, and much harder deployment.

**Second Law: "Why is more important than how."**

*Corollary:* if you only document *how* a system is built and not *why*, future teams will question the design, misunderstand its trade-offs, and risk taking apart its strengths.

*Example:* any architect can read an unfamiliar system's diagrams and work out how its services talk. Without <Term id="adr">Architecture Decision Records (ADRs)</Term> or other recorded context, they can't tell why a particular database, boundary or integration was chosen over the alternatives.

## The "circle of life": architects and developers together

The traditional model, where an architect hands a finished blueprint to developers, is called a catastrophic anti-pattern: a common habit that does serious harm. Instead, architecture and design form a synchronized **circle of life**:

- architects stay hands-on, writing code and building proofs of concept;
- developers understand the architectural constraints, so their class designs fit them;
- and architects avoid the **bottleneck trap**: they don't take ownership of framework code on the <Term id="critical-path">critical path</Term>, because then the whole team waits for them.

### Staying hands-on without becoming the bottleneck

An architect who stops coding becomes an "ivory tower" architect. The lecture gives four ways to stay hands-on, all off the critical path:

1. **Targeted feature delivery.** Build non-critical business features planned one to three iterations ahead. Never touch critical-path framework code.
2. **High-quality proofs of concept.** Write production-quality <Term id="poc">POCs</Term> to compare competing tools. Even throwaway code should be good enough to serve as a reference.
3. **Technical debt stories.** Take low-priority <Term id="technical-debt">technical-debt</Term> or infrastructure tasks that won't stall the team if they slip.
4. **Developer tooling and automation.** Build command-line utilities, custom linters, code generators and automated fitness functions that help the team.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common mistake">
Mixing up an architecture decision with a design principle. "The UI cannot query the database" is a decision: breaking it is a violation. "Prefer asynchronous messaging" is a principle: you may deviate with a good reason. And documenting *how* without *why* breaks the Second Law.
</Callout>
